Prior work has attributed hallucinations in LVLMs to factors such as limitations of the vision backbone or the dominance of the language component, yet the relative importance of these factors remains unclear. To resolve this ambiguity, We propose HalluScope , a benchmark to better understand the extent to which different factors induce hallucinations. Our analysis indicates that hallucinations largely stem from excessive reliance on textual priors and background knowledge, especially information introduced through textual instructions. To mitigate hallucinations induced by textual instruction priors, we propose HalluVL-DPO , a framework for fine-tuning off-the-shelf LVLMs towards more visually grounded responses. HalluVL-DPO leverages preference optimization using a curated training dataset that we construct, guiding the model to prefer grounded responses over hallucinated ones. We demonstrate that our optimized model effectively mitigates the targeted hallucination failure mode, while preserving or

improving performance on other hallucination benchmarks and visual capability evaluations. We will release our HalluScope benchmark and its construction pipeline, along with all components of HalluVL-DPO , including the training data, dataset construction pipeline, and fine-tuned model checkpoints. 2 Related work Evaluating Hallucinations in LVLMs. Hallucinations in LVLMs manifest in several forms. In open-ended text generation, hallucination commonly refers to producing content that is not grounded in the visual input. This is typically evaluated using metrics such as CHAIR [ 34 ] , and more recently SHR [ 51 ] and ALOHa [ 29 ] . Another form of hallucination arises when the model generates incorrect attributes for objects in the image, as evaluated by benchmarks such as MMHalBench [ 38 ] . In closed-ended settings, where prompts query the presence or properties of specific objects, hallucinations are defined as incorrect responses. This form is commonly assessed using benchmarks such as POPE [ 17 ] and HallusionBench [ 10 ] . While these benchmarks measure outcome-level correctness, they provide limited insight into the relative importance of different causes of hallucinations. Mitigating Hallucinations in LVLMs. Previous studies to address hallucinations can be grouped into two main categories: post-hoc (training-free) methods and optimization-based methods. Traini

ng-free approaches are based on different hypotheses that motivate their strategies: Detecting and intervening on “hallucination attention heads” [ 47 ] , re-distributing attention away from semantically uninformative, i.e., sink , tokens [ 13 ] , steering hidden representations [ 21 , 28 , 48 ] , leveraging intermediate representations to detect and suppress hallucinations [ 40 , 39 ] , accumulating and reinforcing visually relevant information [ 1 , 5 ] , contrasting the outputs when given the original and noised image [ 15 ] and amplifying attention to visual tokens and contrasting logits between visual and text-only inputs [ 22 ] fall into the first category. Such methods typically increase inference time [ 15 ] or require modifications to the model’s implementation (e.g., attention calculations [ 47 ] ), and are inherently limited to the hypotheses they consider [ 13 ] , leaving other sources of hallucination unaddressed. Optimization-based methods address hallucinations more fundamentally by directly adjusting the LVLM’s weights, allowing the model to systematically align its outputs with grounded visual information and learned preferences. [ 19 ] proposes a large-scale instruction-tuning dataset including samples with misleading and hallucination-inducing instructions. Another line of work frames hallucination as a preference selection problem and addresses it by optimizing the model on a dataset that provides relevant preference signals [ 51 , 52 , 25 , 45 , 49 , 9 ,

Our pipeline, inspired by [ 43 ] , uses [ 7 ] for image generation and Grounding-DINO [ 23 ] for visual verification. Each dataset sample consists of an image, a textual instruction, and a pair of responses: a preferred, visually-grounded chosen answer ( y w y_{w} ) and a hallucinated, rejected answer ( y l y_{l} ). We focus on three primary task types: • Presupposition Questions: Queries asking about an object’s attributes while implicitly assuming its presence. These are categorized as True Presupposition Questions (TPQ) when the object exists in the image and Counterfactual Presupposition Questions (CPQ) when it is absent. They are are generated via GPT5-mini . • Object Existence: POPE-style questions directly querying the presence or absence of objects. • Detailed Descriptions: Open-ended queries requiring comprehensive scene analysis. The responses are generated such that the chosen ones are more visually grounded (i.e., less hallucinated) than the rejected ones. To induce or prevent hallucinations, the model is prompted wi

th either a correct hint or an incorrect hint , indicating the presence or absence of one or more objects, appended to the original instruction. We refer to this as the unilateral hint augmentation strategy . If the resulting response is far from the original output in a sentence embedding space (determined via a predefined threshold), the sample is included in the preference dataset. Notably, this strategy keeps the distribution of training dataset responses close to the model being optimized, which is crucial for an efficient DPO [ 49 ] . See Section C.2 for further details on the pipeline. Example samples from the dataset are illustrated in Figure 4 .
