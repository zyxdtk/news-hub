Abstract
With the vast amount of content uploaded every hour, along with the AI generated content that can include hallucinations, Automated Fact-Checking (AFC) has become increasingly vital, as it is infeasible for human fact-checkers to manually verify the sheer volume of information generated online. Professional fact-checkers have identified several gaps in existing AFC systems, noting a misalignment between how these systems operate and how fact-checking is performed in practice. In this paper, we introduce CAAFC (Chronological Actionable Automated Fact-Checker), a framework designed to bridge these gaps. It surpasses SOTA AFC and hallucination detection systems across multiple benchmark datasets. CAAFC operates on claims, conversations, and dialogues, enabling it not only to detect factual errors and hallucinations, but also to correct them by providing actionable justifications supported by primary information sources. Furthermore, CAAFC can update evidence and knowledge bases by incorporating recent and contextual information when necessary, thereby enhancing the reliability of fact verification.

The Actionable Justifier:
This module is new to the AFC pipeline and integrates the claim, the retrieved evidence, and the atomic claims with their respective justifications to generate a combined actionable justification. In the context of AFC, actionability refers to producing an explanation that not only highlights the specific errors present in the claim but also provides corrections for those errors and its sources. The prompt used in this module is shown in Figure

The Actionability Evaluator:
To ensure that the generated justification is actionable, this module evaluates its quality using FinGrAct
Eldifrawi
et al.
(
2025
)
. The interaction between this module, the justification revisory module, and the actionable justifier module ensures actionability is produced within the justifications. In FinGrAct, a score of zero mostly indicates that the claim is true and does not require an actionable explanation. FinGrAct evaluates the actionability of a justification based on three criteria. The first criterion assesses whether the justification highlights all factual errors in the claim: a score of 0 indicates that it does not detect any errors, 1 indicates partial detection, and 2 indicates that all errors in the claim are

correctly identified. The second criterion evaluates whether the justification corrects the identified errors. A score of 0 means no corrections are provided, 1 means partial correction, and 2 means that all errors are fully corrected. The third criterion measures whether the information sources cited in the evidence are functional, relevant, and supportive of the justification. A score of 0 indicates that the links are non-functional, 1 that they are functional, 2 that they are relevant, and 3 that they fully support the justification. The total FinGrAct score is obtained by summing the scores from these criteria, resulting in a maximum score of 7 points.
Therefore, if the justification’s actionability score is at least 4 out of 7 points, the justification is considered to have an acceptable degree of actionability. If the justification fails to meet this threshold, the module identifies its weaknesses and provides feedback to the justification revisory, prompting an enhanced and more actionable revision. This refinement process aims to achieve a score of 4 points or more on the actionability scale.

This new module refines the generated justification by incorporating feedback from FinGrAct, with the goal of improving its actionability. Specifically, it enhances the justification by adding missing corrections and highlighting previously unaddressed factual errors. Conclusion
We introduce CAAFC, a framework designed to breakdown AFC into simpler subtasks. CAAFC consistently outperforms SOTA models across multiple datasets, and can be applied to detect and correct hallucinations in LLMs generations. In addition, CAAFC produces actionable, and chronological justifications, supported by reliable sources, thereby enhancing transparency, and explainability.

A.10
Concern about the aggregation of data labels in fine-grained datasets
Because CAAFC is evaluated on datasets that adopt heterogeneous labeling schemes, label aggregation is necessary to produce a unified and consistent output space across benc

hmarks. For example, labels such as misleading, partially true, and false are consolidated into a single false category. This harmonization is a methodological requirement rather than a design choice intended to favor CAAFC.
We acknowledge the concern that such aggregation might appear to create an unfair comparison with state-of-the-art (SOTA) baselines evaluated under the original fine-grained labels. However, the opposite scenario also occurs. In the case of CoverBench and the sampled DiaHalu—both contains only two labels (true or false)—CAAFC is capable of producing an additional unverifiable prediction. This introduces a structural disadvantage for CAAFC, as any unverifiable output is effectively penalized under the binary evaluation scheme.
Despite this clear disadvantage, CAAFC still surpasses the SOTA baselines on CoverBench and the sampled DiaHalu (2 out of 4 datasets). This demonstrates that the observed performance gains cannot be attributed to label aggregation.
Furthermore, we provide detailed justifications explaining why each claim is deemed true, false, or unverifiable, compensating for the lack of fine-grained classification.
