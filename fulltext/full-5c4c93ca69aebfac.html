While traditional autograding pipelines efficiently check functional correctness via unit tests, they struggle with multi-criteria evaluation and pedagogically useful feedback. Recent work explores hybrid approaches using LLMs to approximate richer instructor feedback (Wang et al. , 2024 ; Pereira and Ferreira Mello, 2025 ) . In programming assignments, several frameworks arose that treat LL

Ms as structured graders constrained by rubrics. CodEv combines Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting with LLM ensembles and agreement tests (Tseng et al. , 2024 ) , while other approaches fine-tune LLMs to improve grading consistency (Yousef et al. , 2025 ) . Classroom deployments show mixed results: Mark My Works found no significant correlation between AI and human scores in a 191-student pilot, with AI grading more conservatively (Qiu et al. , 2026 ) , whereas multi-course evaluations using structured rubrics report high AI–instructor correlation (Bernik et al. , 2025 ) . Prompting strategy significantly affects grading quality. CoT prompting improves interpretability and alignment with expert evaluation (Akyash et al. , 2025 ) , while question-specific rubrics enhance agreement, with studies introducing leniency metrics to quantify systematic strictness differences (Pathak et al. , 2025 ) . Work comparing LLM judgments to human graders confirms that prompt engineering and careful feature selection position LLMs as complementary tools rather than replacements (Grandel et al. , 2024 ) . Benchmarking studies reveal substantial model-dependent differences in accuracy (Mohamed et al. , 2025 ) , and feedback generation, while effective, may include hallucinated issues (Jacobs and Jaschke, 2024 ) . Beyond programming, LLMs have been evaluated for short-answer grading (Wangwiwattana and Tongvivat, 2023 ) and essay scoring (Pack et al. , 2024 ; Gaggioli et al. , 2025 ) . Results vary

by domain, as an example: medical education comparisons show mixed effects of rubric provision on agreement metrics, while essay scoring exhibits temporal fluctuations and weak cross-replication stability (Bolgova et al. , 2025 ) . Fine-tuning via parameter-efficient methods (LoRA, QLoRA) improves scoring accuracy and feedback similarity to experts (Katuka et al. , 2024 ) , and specialized datasets such as EngSAF demonstrate successful real-world deployment (Aggarwal et al. , 2025 ) . Overall, LLM-based assessment can range from high alignment to substantial divergence depending on rubric quality, prompt design, and domain. The empirical evidence concentrates on programming assignments and essays, leaving systematic evaluation of command-line assessment unexplored. 2.2 Quantifying Agreement Between Humans and AI Human–AI agreement studies frame grading as a measurement problem, quantifying whether model scores are interchangeable with human judgments (Wang et al. , 2025 ; Pecuchova et al. , 2025 ; Emirtekin and Özarslan, 2026 ) . The choice of metric must match the data type and intended decision. For categorical outcomes (pass/fail, grade bands), chance-corrected measures such as Cohen’s κ \kappa distinguish substantive concordance from chance agreement. For ordinal scales, Weighted Kappa (WK) credits near-matches while penalizing large discrepancies (Emirtekin and Özarslan, 2026 ) . Multi-rater settings employ Fleiss’ κ \kappa or Krippendorff’s α \alpha alongside error prof

iles (Pecuchova et al. , 2025 ) . For continuous scores, Pearson correlation captures linear association between AI and human scores, while Spearman correlation evaluates whether both evaluators preserve the same rank ordering even when the relationship is not strictly linear (Freedman et al. , 2007 ; Spearman, 1904 ) . Also, Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) assesses both absolute agreement and consistency in relative ordering (Shrout and Fleiss, 1979 ) , with different model specifications (ICC(2,1), ICC(3,1)) addressing whether raters are random or fixed. Beyond agreement coefficients, psychometric frameworks using generalizability theory and many-facet Rasch modeling separate variance sources and analyze rater severity (Wang et al. , 2025 ) , while related work evaluates AI-supported rating for replicability (Pillet et al. , 2025 ) . Agreement metrics should also be interpreted alongside fairness considerations, as discrepancies may manifest systematically across subgroups (Arshad et al. , 2025 ) , and stakeholder acceptance, since students may prefer human grading even when AI scores are accurate (Thomas et al. , 2026 ) . For human vs AI analysis, Bland–Altman is especially informative because, unlike Fleiss’ κ \kappa , ICC, or simple correlations, it directly tests whether AI and human scores are practically interchangeable by exposing systematic bias and the limits of agreement across the score range (Martin Bland and Altman, 1986 ) . 3 Materials and Methodology

This taxonomy, known as CogTax, represented in Figure 2 and proposed previously in (Alonso-Carracedo et al. , 2026 ) , ranges from basic information retrieval operations to complex tasks involving the integration of multiple concepts and inter-process communication. CogTax comprises four different categories designed following the principles of Bloom’s Taxonomy. Since its inception in 1956 and subsequent revision in 2001, Bloom’s Taxonomy has been widely adopted across various disciplines, including computer science, to guide curriculum design and evaluation (Machanick, 2000 ; Quan et al. , 2017 ) . Level 1: Information Query / Observation. L1 commands retrieve or display information without modifying system state. They are read-only by design: no file is created, no process altered, no permission changed. Commands in this level include ls , cat , pwd , echo , or cal . Cognitively, L1 commands require vocabulary recognition and basic shell syntax. Operationally, they are reversible by definition. L1 commands form the safe exploration zone of any computing course. Students can interact with the system freely, build mental models of directory structure and file content, and develop pattern recognition without risk of irreversible change. Assessments at L1 test whether students can read system state and interpret command output.

Level 2: Basic Modifications. L2 commands introduce well-defined side effects: creating, copying, moving, or removing files; setting variables; applying simple redirections. Commands in this level include mkdir , cp , mv , or passwd . Cognitive complexity is moderate (students must model preconditions and postconditions); operational impact is bounded and mostly reversible. L2 commands introduce state management and marks the beginning of functional thinking about transformations. Students learn to reason about what a command requires as input and what it produces as output. Assessments at L2 test whether students can predict the postcondition of a command from its precondition. Level 3: Structural Understanding. L3 commands involve multiple operators, conditional logic, pipelines, or permission structures. Commands in this level include chmod , find or simple pipelines ( command1 | command2 ). Cognitive complexity is high (composing multiple primitive concepts); operational impact may be significant. L3 commands represent the productive challenge zone for intermediate learners. They require both conceptual integration and operational awareness which are essential for understanding how combined effects propagate through the system. Level 4: Advanced System Management. L4 commands are deeply nested, multi-concept operations with high cognitive and operational complexity. Commands in this level include kill , scp or background process management ( & ). Bland-Altman analysis was performed to assess systematic bias and limits of agreement between evaluators (Martin Bland and Altman, 1986 ) . With three evaluators, Bland-Altman comparisons are performed pairwise and summarized across evaluator pairs. This approach plots the difference between paired evaluator scores against their mean, enabling visualization of whether disagreements are consistent across the scoring range or concentrated at particular performance levels. A one-category discrepancy represents a less severe disagreement than a multi-category one. These metrics credit near-matches while penalizing large deviations more heavily, making them especially suited to educational scales where

Classic Cohen’s Kappa is designed for exactly two raters, when three or more evaluators are involved, it cannot be applied directly and pairwise computation becomes necessary. Rather than treating all disagreements equally, Weighted Kappa assigns partial credit when categorizations are close but not identical, and pairwise scores are subsequently summarized across all evaluator pairs. Quadratic weighting was employed, which increasingly penalizes larger discrepancies. This metric is particularly valuable in educational assessment contexts because it treats nearby grades as more similar than distant ones, assigning progressively greater penalties as the discrepancy between ratings increases.
