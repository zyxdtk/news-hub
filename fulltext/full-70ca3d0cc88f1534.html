This growing need for deployment-relevant behavioral assessment is further motivated by the practical realities of frontier LLM development: as model scale, proprietary data, and training infrastructure increasingly place direct model intervention beyond the reach of most academic researchers, rigorous behavioral assessment under deployment-relevant conditions becomes a particularly high-leverage direction for scientific contribution. Therefore, we propose Green Shielding, an overarching research agenda for developing user-centric, evidence-backed guidance for how LLMs should be used in real deployments, analogous to an instruction manual that customers would expect for any commercial product. We advance this agenda by studying how benign, deployment-realistic variation in user inputs shapes model behavior and by operationalizing measurable notions of success to ground future user-facing guidance. Rather than prescribing best practices, we focus on establishing the conceptual and empirical basis required to derive them, treating LLMs as fixed, deployed products and analyzing their behaviors under realistic, non-adversarial input variation. This perspective complements red-teaming, which predominantly probes adversarial conditions, by centering the stability properties, utility tradeoffs, and user-relevant risks that emerge in e

veryday use. Building empirical foundations to characterize these everyday-use behaviors is increasingly necessary in high-stakes domains such as healthcare, education, and law, where unreliable outputs can directly affect consequential decisions and public trust [ 9 , 49 ] . This need is further underscored by a rapidly evolving governance landscape, including comprehensive regulation such as the EU AI Act [ 2 , 50 ] and formal evaluation guidance such as NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework [ 45 ] . Green Shielding can also help bridge the three competing sociotechnical imaginaries of modern AI [ 34 ] : frontier AI, which treats AI as a technical artifact; responsible AI, which treats AI as a sociotechnical system; and pragmatic AI, which centers impacts on people’s rights and regulation. We envision Green Shielding as moving beyond frontier-style evaluation by producing deployment-relevant evidence that can inform both organizational practices and regulatory oversight. Our formulation in this paper is informed by Veridical Data Science [ 56 ] , using the Predictability, Computability, and Stability (PCS) framework as a guiding lens for designing empirical procedures that are aligned with the task of interest, feasible under practical constraints, and robust to realistic sources of heterogeneity. Concretely, we propose three core criteria for building a reliable empirical foundation for user-centric guidance within Green Shielding, which we formalize as the CUE criteria : •

Context: Tasks that represent authentic real-world use. • Utility: Reference standards and metrics that represent task-relevant utility. • Elicitation: Perturbation regimes that represent how variation in user inputs and interactions elicits different behaviors. Together, these criteria provide an initial empirical foundation for Green Shielding and guide the study design in this paper. We instantiate this approach in medical diagnosis with LLMs, a high-stakes domain in which model behavior is strongly influenced by everyday differences in user queries. As a first step, we study prompt-level sensitivity in a controlled setting using two widely adopted medical benchmarks, MedQA [ 25 ] and MedXpertQA [ 62 ] . Using open-ended generation as the baseline, we perturb prompts along several practical dimensions—content, format, and tone—and find that these changes consistently shift response correctness on both benchmarks. At the same time, these experiments highlight a limitation of static, exam-style benchmarks for free-form medical assessment: they are designed to be information-complete and single-answer, unlike real-world queries, which are often noisy, vague, and underspecified. This motivates benchmarks and evaluation procedures that better capture diagnostic uncertainty and the need to consider multiple plausible diagnoses. Second, we define structured reference standards and evaluation metrics suited to free-form diagnostic outputs. Instead of relying on the accompanying physician replies, we use multiple frontier LLMs to generate a small set of reference diagnoses for each query, organized into plausible, highly likely, and safety-critical categories. These references support metrics that assess clinically meaningful properties of a model’s differential diagnosis list, including structural metrics such as precision over plausible diagnoses, coverage of likely and safety-critical conditions, and differential breadth (i.e., the number of diagnoses proposed), as well as semantic metrics such as evidence grounding and epistemic uncertainty. Third, to isolate the impact of routine prompt variation, we introduce a prompt neutralization module that identifies common user-level factors and rewrites each query into a standardized, objective medical description, removing these factors while preserving clinical content. However, coverage of the highly likely and safety-critical reference sets remains substantially lower, indicating that even frontier models do not reliably surface the most probable or cannot-miss conditions. Prompt neutralization induces a consistent and interpretable tradeoff across models: differentials become more concise and plausibility increases, but coverage of highly likely and safety-critical diagnoses declines. Comparisons with clinician responses show that physicians produce markedly narrower differentials, prioritizing a small number of leading hypotheses rather than exhaustive enumeration. Taken together, these results demonstrate that routine, non-adversarial differences in prompt formulation can substantially shift clinically meaningful properties of model outputs, and that our framework makes the resulting precision–coverage tradeoffs explicit rather than obscured by single-score evaluations. We do not interpret any single prompting condition as uniformly preferable; instead, different conditions correspond to different operating points, whose desirability depends on context, risk tolerance, and downstream decision-making. By making these tradeoffs measurable, our results motivate human-centered evaluation of usefulness, safety, and trust under realistic interaction settings. Broadly speaking, our experiments support the central premise of Green Shielding

: in non-adversarial settings, ordinary differences in query formulation can produce consistent and measurable changes in model behavior. By combining realistic patient queries with structured reference standards and clinically grounded metrics, our framework enables rigorous and scalable measurement of these effects. As a standalone resource, HCM-Dx bridges the gap between conventional exam-style medical benchmarks and the ambiguity of real-world patient queries in a structured and efficient way. Although we focus on medical diagnosis as a case study in this work, Green Shielding extends naturally to other decision-support domains and, in future work, to multi-turn and more agentic settings, where interaction itself becomes a central determinant of downstream behavior. Prompt-level red-teaming has revealed vulnerabilities such as jailbreaking [ 37 , 60 , 53 , 42 ] and prompt injection [ 18 , 32 ] , and has in turn motivated a growing body of empirical defenses and mitigation strategies [ 27 , 10 ] . Other forms of attacks also consider data poisoning [ 17 , 13 ] and exploiting system-level vulnerabilities in tool-augmented settings [ 57 , 11 , 61 , 14 ] . These efforts play a critical role in risk assessment, governance, and reg

ulatory compliance [ 50 , 2 , 45 ] . At the same time, its emphasis on worst-case probing leaves open the complementary question of how natural, non-adversarial shifts in user inputs translate into changes in utility, stability, and user-facing risk. This body of work largely aligns with what we call AI Safety I, whereas our focus is on AI Safety II: reliability and user-facing risk under realistic, non-adversarial deployment conditions. Prompt Sensitivity of LLMs. Recent work has begun to systematically study prompt sensitivity of LLMs, showing that model outputs can vary substantially under minor semantics-preserving changes in how inputs are presented. Existing efforts include general evaluation frameworks and metrics for quantifying sensitivity [ 59 , 8 , 39 ] , as well as empirical analyses of common instability sources such as prompt formatting [ 40 , 35 ] , the ordering of options in multiple-choice settings [ 38 , 20 ] , and user-provided preference or belief signals that can induce sycophantic behavior [ 41 , 15 , 23 ] . Ceballos-Arroyo et al. [ 7 ] specifically study prompt sensitivity in clinical settings, but their analysis is largely limited to classification, information extraction, and knowledge-matching tasks. Our work builds on known prompt-level factors, but focuses on those commonly encountered in real user interactions, adopts simple and intuitive sensitivity measures, and centers on open-ended medical diagnosis as a more challenging and realistic setting.

Medical Benchmarks for LLMs. The majority of widely used medical benchmarks, including MedQA [ 25 ] , MMLU (clinical knowledge) [ 21 ] , PubMedQA [ 26 ] , MedMCQA [ 36 ] , or a combination of multiple benchmarks [ 44 ] , follow a standardized question-answer style with provided ground truth answers, and usually adopt a multiple-choice format. More recent work has begun to move beyond single-answer medical QA. For example, Tu et al. [ 48 ] evaluates model-generated differential diagnosis lists using top- k k accuracy against expert-validated labels, while HealthBench [ 4 ] also aims to reflect realistic healthcare use cases but does so through synthetic health conversations paired with physician-written, case-specific rubrics. Other benchmarks, such as MedXpertQA [ 62 ] , treat diagnosis as one category within a conventional single-answer QA format. In contrast, our diagnosis case study is built from fully realistic patient queries and targets a narrower but more clinically grounded setting. We focus specifically on diagnostic reasoning over real-world inputs, introduce fine-grained metrics tailored to differential diagnosis, and rely on limited expert supervision to keep the evaluation scalable and broadly usable. We select MedQA and MedXpertQA as representative benchmarks for our preliminary study, as they include substantial diagnostic content and span different difficulty levels. As the need to evaluate LLM capabilities grows and human labeling remains costly, LLM-as-a-judge [ 31 , 19 ] has become a widely used procedure for producing scalable evaluation signals for open-ended generation. While LLM judges can exhibit systematic artifacts, including self-preference bias [ 55 , 52 ] and position bias [ 43 ] , prior works have shown that, with careful prompt design, guardrails, and calibration, they can provide reliable and reproducible measurements in evaluation and benchmarking settings [ 58 , 12 , 29 , 46 ] . In the medical domain, recent works such as MedHELM [ 5 ] has also started to heavily rely on LLM judges during evalution. In our framework, we use multiple LLM judges primarily for (i) constructing structured reference diagnosis sets and (ii) matching clinical synonyms during evaluation; we additionally leverage them for smaller, text-level operations such as paraphrasing and parsing, which are comparatively straightforward for the frontier models we use as judges. For each raw query, an LLM-based annotator produces a concise third-person clinical case description and poses a single diagnostic question, which we denote by x ′ x^{\prime} , while preserving the underlying medical content. We then use a semantic verifier to compare the structured clinical representation x ∗ x^{*} with x ′ x^{\prime} and check that core clinical information is retained. In addition, the annotator outputs a structured set of binary factor labels capturing common user-level variations observed in real interactions. Similar to our pilot study in Section 3 , we organize the annotated factors into the same three broad categories, as shown in Figure 3 . Content-level factors capture variation in the substantive information provided by users, including the presence of specific diagnostic guesses, irrelevant non-medical details, and indicators of missing clinical information such as lack of objective data and incomplete symptom history. Our format-level factor captures how well the request is structured, flagging queries that are ambiguous or unstructured, such as those that combine multiple intents including diagnosis, treatment, and advice-seeki

ng within a single query. Tone-level factors capture how users convey affective state and personal framing, rather than introducing new clinical evidence. We include two such factors: emotional or urgent tone, reflecting subjective distress such as fear, anxiety, panic, or emotional distress; and first-person perspective, indicating whether the query is framed from the point of view of the patient or someone with a close relationship to the patient. Although almost all HCM-Dx queries are written in the first person (99.6%), we nevertheless include it as a formal factor to capture this defining characteristic of real-world patient queries. Not all annotated factors are directly perturbed in our neutralization pipeline. In particular, lack of objective data and lack of symptom history primarily function as indicator factors that characterize missing clinical information in the original query, rather than removable stylistic attributes that can be directly neutralized. We annotate these factors to capture the information state of real-world patient prompts and to support downstream analysis, but our prompt converter does not explicitly edit them. Instead, as a core mechanism for Green Shielding, the converter performs joint neutralization over the perturbable factors while preserving the underlying clinical scenario, enabling a controlled comparison between raw and fully neutralized prompts to quantify the aggregate effect of routine user-level variation on model behavior. Discussion and Limitations Our results offer initial support for Green Shielding as a user-centric agenda for developing evidence-backed guidance for LLM use in deployment. In the medical diagnosis setting, realistic variation in how users present the same underlying case can systematically shift model behavior, revealing tradeoffs that are not captured by single-answer accuracy alone. At the same time, this case study highlights important limitations and suggests concrete directions for future work. It also shows how Green Shielding can be studied empirically through procedures guided by the Predictability, Computability, and Stability (PCS) framework. PCS as a Guiding Framework In this work, the PCS framework [ 56 ] provides a methodological lens for designing and interpreting our empirical study of Green Shielding. Within this lens, the CUE criteria offer a complementary, task-level structure centered on authentic context, task-relevant utility, and realistic elicitation of model behavior. They are closely connected: Context and Utility support Predictability by aligning the study with real-world tasks and meaningful outcomes, while Elicitation supports Stability by enabling systematic analysis of routine input variation. Computability is reflected in the scalable design of our pipeline, including LLM-assisted reference construction, automated normalization and matching, and efficient use of expert annotation. Together, PCS provides a principled methodological foundation,

while CUE helps translate that foundation into a deployment-relevant, scalable, and robust empirical design. Although our case study focuses on medical diagnosis, the same combination can generalize naturally to other domains such as education, law, and public policy, where users often provide open-ended, context-rich inputs and model outputs may inform high-stakes decisions. Interpreting the Observed Tradeoffs It is important to note that we do not interpret the neutralized prompt condition as universally better than the raw one. Instead, our results reveal systematic tradeoffs across metrics such as plausibility, coverage, and breadth, with different prompt conditions occupying different operating points. Which point is preferable may depend on the user, task, and deployment context, especially in high-stakes settings where caution, comprehensiveness, and selectivity may need to be balanced differently. An important direction for future work is therefore not only to improve performance along these dimensions, but also to identify additional task-relevant evaluation criteria, such as uncertainty calibration, actionability, and downstream decision utility, and to determine how these tradeoffs should be resolved in practice. In particular, selecting an appropriate operating point will likely require incorporating stakeholder-specific preferences and risk tolerances, for example through clinician- or user-informed utility functions that weight different types of errors and omis

sions differently. Broad Relevance Although we instantiate Green Shielding through a medical diagnosis case study, the underlying agenda goes beyond medicine. More generally, we study how benign but realistic variation in user presentation affects system behavior in settings where users seek decision support under uncertainty. Similar interaction-dependent instabilities arise in domains such as legal and policy decision-making, education, and recommendation, where outputs are often open-ended, context-dependent, and not well captured by a single correct answer. This perspective is especially relevant in the recent shift toward agentic AI systems. As models increasingly operate as assistants that plan, act, and adapt over extended interactions, user-agent interaction itself becomes a central determinant of downstream behavior. In these settings, small differences in how users express goals, uncertainty, or context may propagate through multi-step reasoning or action pipelines and lead to materially different outcomes, with profound implications for safety, security, and privacy. Limitations of Task Formulation A central limitation of our diagnosis case study is that many real patient queries are highly unstructured and clinically underspecified. Similar forms of underspecified, user-driven input arise in other domains such as education and legal or policy decision-making, where a student may seek help without clarifying their current understanding or specific point of confusio

n, or a user may request legal or policy guidance without specifying the jurisdiction, procedural stage, or relevant factual background. In such cases, requesting a differential diagnosis from the initial query alone may be of limited value, because the input often lacks the information needed for a meaningful assessment. This creates a risk of premature closure: models may produce confident outputs despite insufficient information, reflecting a broader misalignment between benchmark settings that reward definitiveness and real-world settings that often call for uncertainty acknowledgment, clarification, or deferral. In many cases, the more appropriate response would be to acknowledge insufficient information, ask clarifying questions, or defer diagnostic narrowing. Furthermore, diagnosis is not always the most useful target in these settings; outputs such as triage urgency, disposition, or follow-up questions may be more appropriate. A further limitation is that the current benchmark is single-turn, whereas real diagnostic interactions are iterative. Extending Green Shielding to multi-turn settings is therefore important both for clinical realism and for evaluating whether models can recover from ambiguous or poorly framed initial inputs. Limitations of Evaluation Framework Our evaluation framework improves on much prior work by using fully realistic patient-authored queries and more deployment-relevant metrics for open-ended differential diagnosis, but it still does not cap

ture all dimensions of response quality. It supports scalable analysis of hypothesis coverage and selectivity, but abstracts away other aspects such as questioning strategy, explanation quality, conversational appropriateness, and actionability. For example, conversation-level evaluations, including rubric-based assessments such as those used in HealthBench [ 4 ] , could provide a richer characterization of model behavior, but at substantially lower scalability. Meanwhile, although the starting prompts are fully realistic, the downstream evaluation pipeline still introduces synthetic structure through reference construction, normalization, and semantic matching. These abstractions are important for scalability, but they also create a tradeoff between real-world fidelity and measurement control. This tension is not specific to medical diagnosis: scalable evaluation may also miss important features of real-world use, such as adaptation to user context, incorporation of information revealed across turns, and appropriate requests for clarification before offering guidance. An important direction for future work is therefore to develop evaluation pipelines that better preserve the richness of real-world use while still enabling reliable, scalable, and interpretable comparisons. Expert Disagreement and Normative Uncertainty Our preliminary clinician validation also suggests that expert disagreement is itself a meaningful source of uncertainty. In many cases, disagreement appears to

arise not because the reference sets are clearly flawed, but because the underlying patient queries are underspecified. When the available information is sparse or messy, even human experts must rely on subjective inference when deciding which diagnoses belong in the differential. This observation points to a broader challenge for evaluating decision-support systems: some instability in the labels reflects genuine ambiguity in the task rather than simple annotation noise. Future work should therefore study stability across clinicians, specialties, and institutions, and more explicitly examine which tradeoff profiles are preferred in practice. Toward Interactive Evaluation One natural next step is to extend Green Shielding to multi-turn and more agentic settings, where systems are allowed to ask clarifying questions, recognize missing information, and adapt to underspecified user inputs. Doing so would move the agenda beyond single-shot benchmarking and toward a more general framework for understanding how interaction choices shape model behavior under realistic use. 7  Conclusion In this work, we introduce Green Shielding, a user-centric research agenda for developing evidence-backed deployment guidance by studying how benign, deployment-realistic variation in user inputs shapes model behavior. This challenge arises across many domains in which users seek decision support under uncertainty, including medicine, education, and legal or policy settings. We present the first emp

irical study of this agenda in medical diagnosis. Grounded in the PCS framework and structured through the CUE criteria, our approach emphasizes task relevance, feasibility under practical constraints, and robustness to realistic variation. Using scalable reference construction and fine-grained metrics tailored to differential diagnosis, we show that frontier LLMs exhibit systematic behavioral shifts under deployment-relevant prompt variation, revealing reliability tradeoffs that are largely obscured by conventional benchmarks. We further show that prompt neutralization moves model outputs toward more clinician-like differentials while making explicit the tradeoff between comprehensiveness and selectivity. Beyond documenting these effects, Green Shielding defines a concrete target for future work: improving prompt-level strategies and interaction design against explicit, task-relevant criteria such as plausibility, coverage, and breadth, rather than optimizing only for aggregate accuracy. More broadly, Green Shielding provides a foundation for studying how interaction choices shape model behavior under realistic use. Future work should expand coverage across models and domains, while extending the framework beyond single-shot evaluation to multi-turn and more agentic settings, where systems must recognize missing information, ask clarifying questions, and adapt to underspecified user inputs. In the medical setting, larger clinician studies and complementary conversation-level

assessments will also be needed to strengthen validation and better capture dimensions of response quality beyond differential diagnosis generation alone. We believe Green Shielding can help catalyze a broader shift toward interactive, user-centric evaluation paradigms that better reflect real deployment conditions and provide more actionable guidance for improving AI systems in practice.
