Throughout the figure our query documents are in blue, saturated purple denotes topic relevance, saturated teal denotes solution-summary relevance, and muted colors denote irrelevant documents. 1 Introduction Large language models are increasingly used in mathematical reasoning systems, serving both as natural-language assistants and components of formal reasoning pipelines (Ferrag et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2025 ) . To improve their effectiveness and reliability, modern agentic systems often supplement proof generation with retrieval of relevant mathem

atical knowledge. Information retrieval (IR) (Zhu et al., 2023 ) helps these systems find analogous examples, relevant theorems, solution patterns, formal premises, and definitions that can guide multi-step reasoning, making retrieval a core component of mathematical AI systems. Information retrieval for mathematics Retrieval has become important in both formal theorem proving and in informal mathematical problem solving. In formal settings, IR is used to expose relevant definitions, lemmas, declarations (Yang et al., 2023 ; Shen et al., 2025 ; Khramov et al., 2025 ) , library code (Gao et al., 2024 ; Shen et al., 2025 ) , and previously written proofs to assist a prover or autoformalizer (Liu et al., 2025 ) . In informal settings, it is used to ground answers in trusted mathematical material and to provide analogous examples, solution schemas, or theorem-level context (Levonian et al., 2023 ; Yang et al., 2024 ) . Although these systems differ in what they retrieve and how they retrieve it, they share a common requirement: retrieved documents should be useful for mathematical reasoning, rather than simply textually similar to the query. Yet, such systems rarely justify their choice of retrieval framework, making rigorous evaluation of mathematical retrieval necessary for building more effective mathematical agents. Challenges of evaluating mathematical retrieval Evaluating mathematical retrieval is challenging because related mathematical problems are relatively infrequent,

and their relevance is often not captured by surface similarity. Two problems may use different notation, wording, or terminology while relying on the same method, e.g., an extremal argument, invariant, or auxiliary construction. Conversely, problems with similar objects or formulas may require very different reasoning. Prior work (Su et al., 2025 ; Xiao et al., 2024 ; Alshammari et al., 2026 ) has therefore relied on expert annotation of semantic relevance, but such annotation is costly, hard to scale, and prone to annotator disagreement. Moreover, to keep relevance assessment tractable, many benchmarks use narrow relevance definitions and low-granularity labels (Su et al., 2025 ; Ju & Dong, 2025 ; Alshammari et al., 2026 ) . This penalizes genuinely useful retrievals that do not match the benchmark’s specific notion of relevance. All in all, existing methods thus remain hard to scale to larger corpora and diverse mathematical domains, while preserving a fine-grained notion of relevance. SABER-Math To address this gap, we introduce SABER-Math , the S calable A utomated BE nchmark for R etrieval in Math . To our knowledge, it is the first fully-automated benchmark designed specifically for fine-grained evaluation of mathematical information retrieval without requiring expert annotation. Scalable relevant problem pair discovery To address the issues of rare relevant problem pairs, SABER-Math starts from a large corpus of 283 283 K high-school and olympiad-style problem-solutio

n pairs and uses two scalable complementary relevance signals to find relevant problem pairs. First, we assign each problem topics in a mathematical ontology and use topic overlap to find problems involving related concepts, theorems, or domains. Second, we extract short LLM-generated summaries of each solution’s core idea and use lexical overlap between summaries to find problems with similar solution methods. These signals are not used as final relevance labels. Instead, we use them only for candidate selection: we identify 1000 1000 query problems across Algebra, Geometry, Number Theory, Combinatorics, and Calculus, such that we can pair each query with 150 150 candidate documents spanning different forms of mathematical relatedness. Automated relevance estimation After constructing each query’s candidate set, SABER-Math assigns fine-grained relevance labels for each candidate using pairwise LLM judgments. To make this scalable, we avoid exhaustive all-pairs comparison and instead use a Swiss-style tournament, which prioritizes comparisons between candidates of similar estimated relevance. We aggregate the resulting preferences with a Bradley–Terry model to obtain continuous relevance scores, which serve as ground-truth ratings for our benchmark evaluation. Experimental results We evaluate sparse lexical retrievers such as BM25 and TF-IDF, specialized mathematical retrieval frameworks such as Approach Zero (Zhong et al., 2021 ) , and embedding-based retrievers ranging from

early pretrained encoders to recent state-of-the-art models. Among all evaluated models, recent embedding models substantially outperform everything else, with the Octen open model family (Team, 2025 ) ranking highest for most domains, followed closely by the closed Gemini-Embedding-2 model (Lee et al., 2025 ) . Among newer embedding models, however, we show that performance on general-purpose retrieval benchmarks such as MTEB is not a reliable predictor for mathematical performance, demonstrating the need for our work. We also provide a counterfactual analysis of models’ reliance on mathematical notation, showing that weaker retrievers over-rely on symbols and formulas without capturing their meaning, which hurts their performance. Key contributions Our contributions include: • SABER-Math , a scalable automated benchmark for mathematical information retrieval. • An extensive evaluation of classical, specialized, and embedding-based retrievers, showing that general-purpose retrieval benchmarks do not fully predict mathematical IR performance. • Domain-level and counterfactual analyses of retriever behavior, identifying weaknesses in symbolic domains. • Publicly-available code and data to support reproducibility and further research. 2 Related Work In this section, we discuss related work on evaluating information retrieval, its applications in mathematical reasoning, and the use of LLMs as data processors and judges. General-purpose retrieval benchmarks General-purpose retri

eval benchmarks such as BEIR (Thakur et al., 2021 ) and MTEB (Muennighoff et al., 2023 ) have become standard for comparing lexical, dense, and reranking models across domains and task types. These resources are useful for testing generalization and overall embedding quality, but they mainly measure general-purpose passage relevance: topical overlap, answer containment, or semantic rephrasing. When retrieval necessitates in-depth reasoning, it remains unclear how well general retrieval capabilities transfer to mathematical domains. Agentic workflows with retrieval Agentic LLM systems interleave planning, tool use (Ju et al., 2026 ) , memory (Park et al., 2023 ; Packer et al., 2023 ) , and self-reflection (Madaan et al., 2023 ) , with retrieval serving as a common tool action for grounding intermediate reasoning in external evidence or domain context (Zhu et al., 2023 ; Ferrag et al., 2025 ; Lewis et al., 2020 ; Nakano et al., 2021 ) . In such workflows, retrieval quality matters, as irrelevant or incomplete context can derail subsequent reasoning, while well-targeted retrieval can support completion of harder tasks and reduce hallucinations (Wu et al., 2024 ; Hong et al., 2024 ; Yoran et al., 2024 ) . SABER-Math abstracts away the agentic scaffolding and directly evaluates retriever performance. Retrieval in mathematical reasoning and theorem proving Mathematical retrieval spans a continuum from informal problem similarity to formal premise selection. In proof assistants, acc

urate retrieval has long been studied as premise selection for advancing the formal proof (Irving et al., 2016 ; Bansal et al., 2019 ) . LLM-based provers continue this line: ReProver (Yang et al., 2023 ) and REAL-Prover (Shen et al., 2025 ) retrieve a set of premises conditioned on the formal Lean proof state code. At the same time, newer systems increasingly retrieve over natural-language mathematical statements, even when the final goal is formal reasoning. Gao et al. ( 2024 ) and Liu et al. ( 2025 ) do so by informalizing mathlib4 statements for proving and autoformalization respectively, while Archon (Ju et al., 2026 ) combines informal retrieval with formal search to translate informal arguments into machine-checkable developments. Beyond proof assistants, informal retrieval is also used to ground mathematical question answering, retrieve solution schemas or analogous problems, and construct mathematical knowledge graphs (Levonian et al., 2023 ; Dixit & Oates, 2024 ; Yang et al., 2024 ; Bian et al., 2025 ) . However, these works typically evaluate retrieval only on downstream performance, making it difficult to isolate the quality of the retriever itself. Such measurements conflate retriever quality with the generator’s reasoning ability, prior knowledge, prompt sensitivity, and robustness to irrelevant context. A model may succeed despite poor retrieval or fail despite useful retrieval, so downstream performance alone does not reliably isolate the retriever’s contribut

ion. Our work therefore focuses on informal mathematical IR as a standalone problem. Mathematical retrieval benchmarks and relevance coverage Several recent benchmarks evaluate mathematical or reasoning-intensive retrieval more directly. BRIGHT (Su et al., 2025 ) evaluates mathematical relevance through a shared theorem or solving method, and MathNet (Alshammari et al., 2026 ) studies retrieval of mathematically equivalent or structurally similar Olympiad problems. However, these works rely on human expert annotation, which is costly and difficult to scale to larger corpora or diverse mathematical domains. Other works provide automatic pipelines, with MIRB (Ju & Dong, 2025 ) unifying semantic statement, premise, QA, and formula retrieval, while RAR-b (Xiao et al., 2024 ) converts reasoning datasets, including MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021 ) and GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021 ) , into retrieval over pooled answer candidates. While these benchmarks improve scalability, their construction can still lead to partial relevance coverage. Pooling-based collections leave many query-document pairs unvalidated, so relevant items surfaced by new systems may be treated as irrelevant or receive no credit, potentially introducing false negatives and affecting model comparisons (Buckley & Voorhees, 2004 ; Sakai & Kando, 2008 ) . Recent work shows that missing judgments matter, but also that repairing them reliably is difficult (Upadhyay et al., 2024 ; Abbasiantaeb et al., 2024 ) . This issue is es

pecially acute in mathematics, where useful relationships are sparse and fine-grained: two problems may share a common method without being equivalent. SABER-Math is designed with this problem in mind, as relevance is estimated on a continuous scale, and evaluates retrievers on a task where each document has been sufficiently validated. LLMs as data processors and judges LLMs are increasingly used to process data at scale, including annotation (Bansal & Sharma, 2023 ; Gilardi et al., 2023 ) , preference estimation (Thomas et al., 2024 ) , and evaluation (Zheng et al., 2023 ; Upadhyay et al., 2024 ; Lin et al., 2025 ) . In particular, strong LLM judges can align well with human judgments in open-ended tasks, but also exhibit known biases and therefore require task-specific validation (Zheng et al., 2023 ; Ye et al., 2024 ; Wataoka et al., 2024 ; Dekoninck et al., 2024 ; Shi et al., 2025 ; Saito et al., 2023 ) . In particular, individual pointwise scores can be noisy or non-transitive (Liu et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2024 ) , making it difficult to reliably measure subjective criteria such as relevance (Petrov et al., 2026 ) . To address these issues, prior work has used pairwise comparisons with Bradley-Terry-style aggregation, converting many local preferences into a global ranking that can reduce noise and mitigate bias (Zheng et al., 2023 ; Liu et al., 2024 ; Dekoninck et al., 2024 ) . In SABER-Math , we adopt this framework to generate fine-grained relevance scores, validate

it against human annotations, and show that it is preferable to standard ordinal ranking methods. We also use LLMs more broadly for data processing throughout the benchmark. 3 Methodology In this section, we describe the construction of SABER-Math , our benchmark for evaluating information retrieval in mathematics. SABER-Math is designed to test whether retrieval systems can identify mathematically meaningful relevance between problems, such as shared concepts, reasoning patterns, or solution structure, rather than relying on surface-level textual similarity. We begin by building SABER-Math from a large corpus of high-school-level problems and solutions ( Section ˜ 3.1 ). To identify related problem pairs at scale, we use two complementary automatically extracted relevance signals: topic overlap between problems ( Section ˜ 3.2 ) and lexical similarity between short LLM-extracted solution summaries ( Section ˜ 3.3 ). We then construct reranking tasks by selecting a set of query and candidate problems of varying types and strengths of relatedness ( Section ˜ 3.4 ). Finally, we assign fine-grained relevance ratings to the candidates using an LLM-as-a-judge pairwise preference framework ( Section ˜ 3.5 ), which serve as reference labels for evaluating performance in SABER-Math . We present further data collection and preprocessing details in App. ˜ B . 3.2 Analyzing Problem Pairs using Extracted Topics Overlapping objects, theorems, subfields and topics provide a strong signal of mathematical relevance beyond textual similarity, despite potentially different phrasing across documents. In this section, we explain how we capture this notion by constructing a MathWorld-derived ontology ( Weisstein, ) ( Section ˜ 3.2.1 ), assigning topics to problems, and computing pairwise relevance f

rom topic sets using the hierarchy ( Section ˜ 3.2.2 ). 3.2.1 Mathematical Ontology We represent each problem by a set of mathematical topics and theorems, which provide good signals of mathematical relatedness. Because related topics may differ lexically while remaining close in the subject hierarchy, we use a fixed ontology tree rather than treating topics as a flat set. Ontology tree construction We build our ontology from Wolfram’s MathWorld ( Weisstein, ) , whose entries provide broad coverage of high-school, olympiad, and university mathematics and are linked hierarchically across mathematical areas, concepts, and theorems. We represent each MathWorld topic as a node and each hierarchical link as a directed edge from a more general topic to a more specific one, yielding a directed acyclic graph (DAG). This graph is not generally a tree: a topic can appear along multiple root-to-topic paths because mathematical concepts often belong to several areas. For example, Pascal’s Triangle appears under both a combinatorial branch, Discrete Mathematics → Combinatorics \text{Discrete Mathematics}\rightarrow\text{Combinatorics} , and a number-theoretic branch, Number Theory → Special Numbers \text{Number Theory}\rightarrow\text{Special Numbers} , reflecting different uses and contexts. To preserve this information while obtaining a tree structure, we duplicate nodes with multiple root-to-topic paths. This lets us disambiguate topic assignments by context when annotating problems fr

om our corpus. The conversion increases the ontology size from ∼ 10.5 ​ K \sim 10.5K to ∼ 17.1 ​ K \sim 17.1K nodes, while the maximum root-to-node depth remains 5. 3.2.2 Scoring Problem Pair Relevance based on Ontologies Automatic topic assignment To calculate problem pairs relevance signals, we need to first extract relevant ontology topics for each problem in our mathematical corpus. Due to the size of our ontology, presenting it to an LLM in full is infeasible. Instead, for each problem we do a top-down recursive traversal of the ontology. At each visited ontology node we rely on GPT-OSS-120B (Agarwal et al., 2025 ) to do local relevance scoring. The model is called with the problem statement, solution, and the ontology labels of the node and its children, and is asked to assign each candidate a relevance score, which we then normalize across nodes. We retain candidates whose normalized scores exceed a threshold τ = 0.85 \tau=0.85 and recursively apply the same procedure to their descendants. By having the parent node as a reference, we allow the algorithm to terminate early when the children are substantially less relevant than their parent, preventing redundant computation. Finally, we keep only the most specific assigned topics by removing topic ancestors. As a result, using them as final relevance labels in our benchmark could over-penalize other releva

nt connections between problems. To obtain more fine-grained benchmark ratings, we instead use a pairwise LLM-as-a-judge framework. We aggregate these judgments with a Bradley–Terry model to produce continuous relevance scores, and use a Swiss-style tournament to reduce the computation. Pairwise preference judging with an LLM-as-a-Judge We assign each candidate its final relevance rating using an LLM-as-a-judge framework (Chan et al., 2023 ; Zheng et al., 2023 ; Liu et al., 2024 ; Dubois et al., 2024 ) . Since judging mathematical relevance requires comparing solution ideas, proof structure, and techniques, we use a strong reasoning model, GPT-OSS-120B , as our judge. Rather than eliciting absolute relevance scores, we ask the judge for pairwise preferences, following prior work showing that comparative judgments are more reliable than direct scoring (Li et al., 2024 ) . This finding is also confirmed by our human evaluation experiment in Section ˜ D.1 . For each query problem and its solution, we ask the judge to compare two candidate problems and their solutions and pick the one that is more mathematically relevant to the query. Bradley–Terry estimation of latent relevance To convert judge preferences into continuous relevance scores, similarly to Dekoninck et al. ( 2024 ) , we fit a Bradley-Terry (BT) model separately for each query. Under this model, the probability that candidate i i is judged to be more relevant than candidate j j by the LLM is assumed to be: P ​ ( i >

j ) = π i π i + π j , P(i>j)=\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{i}+\pi_{j}}, where π i \pi_{i} is candidate i i ’s latent relevance score. Scaling pairwise comparisons with a Swiss-style tournament Exhaustive pairwise judging requires n ​ ( n − 1 ) 2 \frac{n(n-1)}{2} LLM calls, which is prohibitively expensive for large reranking sets, and wastes calls on clearly unequal candidates without gaining much information. We therefore use a Swiss-style tournament to select an informative subset of comparisons: 1. Initialization: Candidates paired uniformly at random. 2. Iterative matching: In each subsequent round, each candidate is paired with an unjudged opponent whose cumulative win count is as close as possible. The tournament concentrates judge calls on close comparisons, which are the most informative. We use R = 20 R=20 tournament rounds, as we find this to be sufficient in Section ˜ 4.3 . Further details on how we run the tournament are provided in Section ˜ C.4 . Finally, we fit the BT model by maximizing the likelihood of the obtained pairwise judgments. Appendix A Limitations While SABER-Math provides a scalable framework for evaluating mathematical information retrieval, several limitations remain. First, the benchmark is built primarily from

high-school, olympiad, and early undergraduate-style problems, so performance may not fully transfer to research-level mathematics, formal proof libraries, textbook retrieval, or highly specialized domains. In addition, SABER-Math evaluates reranking over preselected candidate sets rather than open-ended retrieval over the full corpus. This design makes it possible to assign fine-grained relevance judgments to every evaluated candidate, while complementary first-stage retrieval evaluation remains an important extension. Finally, the benchmark uses automated LLM-based processing to support construction at scale. Although this can introduce noise into individual relevance signals, our human validation indicates that the resulting judgments are reliable for comparing retrieval systems. B.1 Gathering Data from Public Forums Large retrieval systems often rely on large-scale curation from public web sources. To establish a broad base for our benchmark, we first collect problems and solutions from the Art of Problem Solving (AoPS) community, which hosts a large collection of mathematical discussions. In the first round, documents were paired uniformly at random. After each round, each document was assigned a running score equal to the number of times the LLM judge preferred the candidate up to that point. Methodology and motivation To investigate whether mathematical notation or surrounding textual content contributes more to retrieving relevant documents, we conduct the following experiment using our benchmark. For each query problem, we separately extract its mathematical expressions and its textual content. We discard queries for which either component is empty, leaving 969 of the original 1000 query problems. For each retrieval method, including both embedding-based and classical approaches, we then compute two relevance estimates for every remaining query. First, we compare the query’s mathematical content with the problem-and-solution text of each of its five highest-rated relevant candidate problems according to the benchmark. We compute the relevance score assigned by the retrieval method for each of these five candidates and take their average. We repeat the same procedure using only the query’s textual content. Thus, each query receives two average relevance scores: one based on its mathematical content and one based on its textual content. Finally, for each query and retrieval method, we record whether the mathematical or textual component receives the higher av

For each retrieval method, we report the percentage of queries for which the mathematical component is judged more relevant than the textual component.
