Related Work
Speculative decoding and tree verification.
Speculative
decoding was introduced by
Leviathan et al. (
2023
)
and
Chen et al. (
2023
)
. SpecInfer
(Miao et al.,
2024
)
generalized single-chain verification to tree-structured candidates,
verifying an entire token tree in one target-model forward pass via an
ancestor-only attention mask — the verification mechanism this paper
reuses unmodified (Section
3.5
).
Autoregressive tree drafters: EAGLE.
The EAGLE
line
(Li et al.,
2024a
,
b
,
2025
)
builds high-quality
draft trees on top of an autoregressive drafter, culminating in
EAGLE-3’s
(Li et al.,
2025
)
training-time-test procedure. Because an
autoregressive drafter’s hidden state

at position
i
i
genuinely depends on
the realized tokens at all earlier positions, branching an EAGLE-style
tree requires re-running the (small, but nontrivial) draft model per
branch — there is no shared, precomputed representation across branches
the way there is for a block-diffusion backbone. Domino, and by extension
our tree, avoid this cost by keeping the expensive backbone pass
parallel and shared, and branching only the cheap sequential correction
(Section
2.3
, Structural Fact B) — a different
point on the cost/branching trade-off than the EAGLE line occupies.
Block-diffusion drafting: DFlash.
DFlash
(Chen et al.,
2026
)
is the parallel drafter backbone Domino, and therefore this paper, builds
on (Section
2.2
). DFlash itself drafts a single
chain per round from its marginal per-position distributions; it does not
build trees.
Marginal draft trees: DDTree.
DDTree
(Ringel and Romano,
2026
)
is the direct algorithmic ancestor of the tree-construction mechanism used
in this paper: a best-first heap over a block-diffusion drafter’s
per-position marginals, verified with an ancestor-only attention mask
(Section
2.4
). Our conditional tree uses the
identical heap mechanism; the entire technical contribution of this paper
is a node-scoring function — Domino’s GRU-corrected, path-dependent
conditional log-probability — that DDTree’s factorized formulation cannot
express, because DDTree only ever consults per-position marginals that do
not depend on which tokens are realized a

long a candidate’s own path
(Section
3.1
).
Cost-aware budgets: CaDDTree.
CaDDTree
(Zhang et al.,
2026
)
replaces DDTree’s fixed, offline-tuned node budget with a per-round greedy
stopping rule, proved optimal under a convexity assumption on verification
cost, for the factorized case (Section
2.5
).
CondAdaptive (Section
3.4
) inlines the same rule
into our conditional tree’s pop loop; the optimality framework plausibly
carries over, but the corrected drafter’s path-probabilities are
miscalibrated as target-acceptance estimates, so the resulting budget
gives no gain over a fixed one (Section
B.3
).
Closest related work: JetSpec.
JetSpec
(Hu et al.,
2026
)
is, to our knowledge, the closest published method to our goal: it builds a
causal draft tree directly on top of a parallel drafter, combining
one-forward-pass drafting efficiency with branch-wise causal conditioning,
by training a new causal parallel draft head aligned with the target
model’s own factorization. The honest distinction is that JetSpec trains a
new draft head end-to-end for tree-structured causal conditioning, whereas
our method is training-free: it reuses Domino’s released, off-the-shelf
correction head, adding no new parameters and requiring no additional
training data or compute. We flag plainly that this is a narrow niche: if a
future revision of JetSpec is shown to be training-free as well, or if it
adds a per-round adaptive budget we could not get CondAdaptive to deliver
(Section
B.3
), the distinction w

e claim narrows
substantially, since a purpose-trained causal head is a strictly more
expressive mechanism than reusing a GRU correction that was not trained
with tree branching in mind.

Limitations and Future Work
We state the current scope limits plainly rather than deferring them to a
closing paragraph.
GPU-native builder: scope.
DominoTree’s default builder is
GPU-resident: heap management is left to Python, but per-node correction
executes via CUDA-graph capture/replay rather than a per-node round-trip
through eager PyTorch, analogous to Domino’s own CUDA-graphed chain
correction (Section
3.3
). What remains future work is
extending this pattern beyond a single-stream research harness: a
multi-request, batched, or serving-integrated (e.g. SGLang/vLLM) version
of the same graph-capture idea.
A6000 and 5080 throughput are not directly comparable.
This
matters for the Qwen3-8B grid (Table
1
), collected on a single
RTX A6000 rather than the two RTX 5080s used for the Qwen3-4B grid this paper
headlines.
Microbenchmarking indicates the A6000’s
verification-forward latency in this setup is both higher and noisier than
the 5080’s, consistent with clock- or attention-kernel-throttling rather
than the 5080’s cleaner memory-bandwidth-bound profile, so absolute
tokens-per-second numbers from the two GPUs should not be compared
directly. Only same-GPU relative comparisons — a tree method vs. its own
chain baseline, or own-AR-normalized speedups — carry across the two grids.
