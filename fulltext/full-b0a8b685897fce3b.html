Introduction Large-language models have proven to be remarkable if inconsistent chameleons of public attitudes and opinions (e.g Argyle et al . 2023 ; Bisbee et al . 2024 ; Santurkar et al . 2023 ; Kozlowski et al . 2024 ). The extent to which LLMs are able to produce accurate surrogates of cultural taste and consumption remains an open empirical question that becomes more urgent by the day, with market research companies already offering provisional “synthetic” survey panels (e.g. Currie 2025 ; VeraSight 2026 ). In this study, we build on past work on silicon sampling by extending considerations of its algorithmic fidelity and alignment to the domain of cultural tastes. We use large-language models from OpenAI, Anthropic, and DeepSeek to produce silicon surrogates of respondents from the Survey of Public Participation in the Arts (SPPA), compare the tastes and preferences from these silicon samples against their SPPA counterparts. First, we evaluate the ecological and relational

ity fidelity of these silicon tastes. Then, using a mixed-effects meta-analysis framework, we evaluate the multivariate associations of silicon tastes with key dimensions of social space. We find the following. (1) Silicon samples have a systematic postive-bias for liking , resulting in inflated ecological estimates of tastes. The individual-level bias of silicon samples are not well-explained by the WEIRD-bias often discussed in the literature. (2) The complex relationality in real taste structures is completely lost among silicon samples. (3) Finally, very little of the known cultural alignment between tastes and social space are preserved. Silicon samples attenuate age-taste associations, resurrect anachronistic class-taste associations, caricaturize gender- and race-taste associations. Assessing the fidelity of such silicon tastes is crucial for two main reasons. First, silicon tastes, if faithful, allow for research designs formerly infeasible. Silicon sampling make a seductive promise to researchers. LLMs answer surveys quickly, cheaply, and without fatigue or complaint. It may now be possible to cheaply and quickly produce surveys of public opinion; to field surveys that were thought to be too long, laborious, or boring; to effectively go back in time and ask and then re-ask the questions that you couldn’t or didn’t think so. For social scientists interested in the study of tastes, silicon samples could facilitate finer-grain investigations than previously possible. Pr

esent surveys of cultural taste tend to rely on responses to fuzzy cultural categories, such as genres. As Vandebroeck ( Vandebroeck 2022 ) points out, this convention assumes (a) that consumers possess a taxonomy of cultural categories that’s more or less similar to the one posited by the survey and (b) that the cultural categories themselves are not the subject of significant contestation and ambiguity. Genres, in other words, are inherently fuzzy, and it is far from obvious that respondents share the taxonomy a survey imposes on them. Yet fielding surveys that probe finer aspects of cultural taste is difficult, since this makes a survey long, tedious, and expensive. Were silicon samples to prove faithful, they would let researchers interrogate taste at the level of specific works, performances, or sub-genres, and to vary the framing of cultural categories experimentally, at a scale and cost that fielding such instruments on human respondents could never match. Further, silicon samples would facilitate interpolated projections of cultural taste in time and space, allowing up to improve our understanding of the ecology of tastes over time. Extrapolation of public opinion surveys into social space with limited support is a dangerous proposition, but interpolation over known spaces may be plausible and rewarding in and of itself. There are no notable panel studies of cultural taste, and few repeated cross-sectional studies of cultural taste ( Ma 2020 ). Silicon sampling may al

low for the construction of such data-sets and produce generative preliminary analysis of changes in taste ecologies over time. Second, silicon sampling of public opinion and behavior is quickly expanding. While the social scientific community has urged caution when it comes to use of silicon samples (e.g. Gao et al . 2025 ; Bisbee et al . 2024 ; Broska et al . 2025 ), these norms are unsettled and frequently transgressed. It’s important to have a better understanding of the quality, biases and limitations of silicon samples across a breadth of sociologically relevant domains ( Lyman et al . 2025 ). Assessing the fidelity of silicon sampling is pressing because the practice is expanding rapidly—in the range of domains to which it is applied, in the sophistication of its methods, and in its rapid expansion by survey and market research companies. Researchers have used LLMs to simulate responses to contentious political items drawn from the American National Election Studies ( Li et al . 2024 ), German public opinions ( Ma et al . 2025 ), and public opinions about climate change ( Lee et al . 2024 ), to project audience attitudes and policy preferences, and to model public-health attitudes such as vaccine acceptance. Importantly, a robust commercial interest in the provision of silicon samples has emerged. Survey and market research companies like Qualtrics and VeraSight have already begun experimental rollouts of “synthetic panels” that are built using silicon samples ( Isaza

2024 ; Currie 2025 ; VeraSight 2026 ). Ordinary survey respondents also frequently turn to LLMs and chatbots to generate ostensibly ‘human’ response surveys, meaning that even researchers who use orthodox survey methods are likely to face non-negligible contamination of ‘synthetic’ responses in their data ( Zhang et al . 2025 ; Veselovsky et al . 2025 ). The techniques themselves behind silicon sampling have also grown more elaborate. Beyond zero-shot prompting, some scholars now fine-tune models on structured survey data to better recover response distributions, attach parameter-efficient persona adapters trained on large persona banks, and query models directly for entire response distributions rather than aggregating individual answers. The ambition of these efforts is captured by the emergence of large reference datasets purpose-built for constructing “digital twins” of individual respondents. Others have used low-ranked adaptations of large foundation models to finetune and produce locally-hosted silicon samples ( Dash et al . 2025 ). The faster silicon sampling diffuses across these settings, the more consequential its hidden biases become, and the more urgent it is to establish where it is, and is not, faithful. 2. Literature review 2.1. Silicon sampling and the study of taste Large language models (LLMs) are probabilistic machine learning models that are used to process and produce textual data. At a high level, LLMs resemble the autocomplete technologies that have be

come on search engines and other digital spaces, but with considerably greater scale and sophistication ( Bail 2024 ). They power the dialogue agents that have already experienced widespread consumer adoption. In this study, we extend ongoing work that applies LLMs to survey research. In particular, we focus on the use of LLMs to simulate synthetic human samples in public opinion surveys, a practice often referred to as silicon sampling . The intuition behind silicon sampling is simple. Homo silicus has advanced to such a state that many LLMs are able to converse and reason like humans, that the prospect of AI passing Turing tests just about seems a fait accompli ( Dillion et al . 2023 ). If homo silicus really was capable of providing such a great facsimile of human responses, then it may be possible to productively sample responses from LLMs, the same way we would from human populations. LLMs by the nature of their training and design are computational models of humans and likely possess a great deal of latent social information ( Horton 2023 ). This latent information can often evince itself negatively in different guises of algorithmic bias, Argyle et al. ( ( 2023 ) ) argue that it may be possible to instead think of them in productive terms as a model of the “complex reflection of the many various patterns of association between ideas, attitudes, and contexts present among humans.” The algorithmic fidelity of LLMs, when appropriately conditioned, means that researchers m

ay be able to induce the model to produce outputs that correlate with the attitudes, opinions, and experiences of distinct human subpopulations. As George Box put it, while all simplified models of human behavior are necessarily wrong, some are useful. So it may be the case for silicon samples of human respondents. Work is also underway exploring the applications of synthetic surrogates of human respondents in domains beyond survey research, from agent-based modeling, to psychological experiments, to behavioral economics experiments, to market research ( Chen et al . 2023 ; Dillion et al . 2023 ; Horton 2023 ; Park et al . 2023 ; Goli and Singh 2024 ). In our study, we advance ongoing work on the robustness and bounds of silicon sampling by applying to cultural fields, in particular exploring the application of silicon sampling to the case of public opinion surveys of cultural taste. Taste in its most literal form refers to gustatory taste, our perception of sweet, salty, bitter, sour, and savory sensations as mediated by a physiologically-defined chemosensory system. From the 16th century and on, however, taste acquired a separate metaphorical meaning as an expression of a person’s aesthetic orientation towards culture, that is, cultural taste. Cultural taste is commonly operationalized as a person’s first-order preferences, feelings, or desires towards an object in a cultural field ( Ma 2024 ). We use the term “silicon tastes” to refer to the tastes and preferences expresse

d by silicon surrogates of human respondents. 2.2. Assessing the fidelity of silicon tastes How faithful are silicon tastes to their carbon counterparts? We contribute to ongoing work examing the bounds and robustness of silicon sampling by examing the quality and fidelity of synthetic cultural tastes. We assess (1) the ecological fidelity of silicon tastes, and whether such validity is mediated by the so-called WEIRD bias of LLMs, (2) the higher-order relations among silicon tastes, and (3) the fidelity of taste-to-social-space associations inferred from silicon samples. We ask three main research questions. (1) How faithful are the ecological estimates of cultural tastes produced by silicon samples? And to what extent are discrepancies in estimates explained by the WEIRD-bias? (2) To what extent do silicon samples preserve the relationality among tastes? (3) To what extent are known cultural associations between tastes and social space preserved among silicon samples? To answer the first question, we compare the ecological estimates of cultural tastes for 17 genres of music from real and silicon samples. Then, we compute and model the Jaccard distance between the real and silicon tastes. To answer the second, we examine and compare the correlation matrices of real and silicon tastes. To answer the third, we model the coefficient bias of silicon samples through a mixed-effects meta-regression. 2.2.1. On the ecological fidelity of silicon tastes. Early work were quick to esta

blish the surprising accuracy of silicon samples, especially when it came to ecological estimates. In their seminal paper, Argyle et al . 2023 first observed “a high degree of correspondence between reported two-party presidential vote choice proportions from GPT-3 and ANES respondents.” For instance, GPT-3 silicon respondents reported an aggregate 0.391 probability of voting for Mitt Romney for the 2012 US Presidential elections; the same percentage from the ANES was 0.404 ( Argyle et al . 2023 ). Bisbee et al. similarly created synthetic surrogates to the ANES, and find “every synthetic mean falls within one standard deviation of the ANES average” ( Bisbee et al . 2024 , pg. 6 ). Studies of silicon samples in domains away from American public opinion of politics have also found success. Brand et al. ( ( 2023 ) ) use a silicon sampling approach to replicate conjoint studies of consumer preference. They report that “[o]verall, the resulting estimates are realistic, both in magnitudes and in distribution … a conjoint-like approach to preference estimation yields results that are strikingly similar to those found in a recent survey of real consumers conducted by Fong et al., as well as to additional human surveys we conduct.” Lee et al . 2024 find they are reasonably good at reproducing ecological estimates of American public opinions towards climate change. Dillion et al.’s ( ( 2023 ) ) likewise find that silicon samples of moral judgements to be surprisingly well-aligned with

those of human participants. The extent to which the same might apply to silicon panels of cultural tastes remains an open empirical question. Further, we ask if discrepancies in ecological fidelity of silicon tastes may be attributable to what we term WEIRD-alignment bias (e.g. Bulté and Rigouts Terryn 2025 ; Liu et al . 2024 ). There can be considerable between-group variance in algorithmic fidelity, with divergences from real data particularly high among groups from communities that are underrepresented in the training data sets of today’s foundation models ( Madden 2025 ; Masoud et al . 2025 ). The unevenness of the training data may result in the systematic misalignment of cultural tastes across the board, such that they are much more faithful for Wealthy, Educated, Industrialized, Rich and Democratic (WEIRD) sub-populations than for others ( Xiao et al . 2025 ; Santurkar et al . 2023 ). We ask if such WEIRD-alignment bias might also explain systematic divergences in the ecological fidelity of silicon tastes. 2.2.2. The relationality of silicon tastes. The fidelity of silicon samples strike many as intuitively implausible; on closer examination, many researchers have uncovered many discrepancies between real survey samples and their silicon counterparts. A persistent finding across studies is that the fidelity of silicon samples are restricted to the coarsest of ecological estimates (e.g. Bisbee et al . 2024 ). Silicon samples are poor at capturing the complex relationalities that exist among persons and non-person subjects in the social world ( Bisbee et al . 2024 ). In Distinction , Bourdieu ( ( 1984 ) ) famously uses the case of taste to illustrate the dialectical relationship between subjective agency and social structure. We ask if the multivariate associations between tastes and social space observed among silicon tastes are faithful to those observed in human survey samples. 3. Data and methods 3.1. Data source The SPPA is a repeated cross-sectional survey that measures cultural attitudes and participation in the United States. From 1982 to 2022, the SPPA included items that tapped individuals’ preferences for music genres, assessing respondents’ likes for a range of musical genres. Due to its longevity and representativeness, the SPPA serves as an effectively “canonical” data that that has provided the empirical basis for an enormous body of research into cultural tastes (e.g. Peterson and Kern 1996 ). 3.2. Generating silicon samples We follow conv

entional approaches to silicon sampling to begin (e.g. Argyle et al . 2023 ; Bisbee et al . 2024 ). For a given survey S S , we use an LLM model L L to generate a data set of matching synthetic responses S ^ \hat{S} . After instructing the LLM model L L to adopt a persona defined by a set of respondent characteristics, we then ask it to answer the same set of prompts contained in S S . We choose respondent personas and cultural taste questions to match questions that human respondents were asked in S S , allowing us to gauge how accurately and precisely the synthetic LLM responses capture actual public opinion. We generate synthetic samples using three different large-language models: (1) GPT 4.0, (2) Claude 3.7 Sonnet, and (3) DeepSeek R1. For each of the models, we set temperature to be 1.0, and constrain the maximum output tokens to 2,500. 3.2.1. Prompt settings We closely follow a style of prompting that has become dominant in political science, in particular when it comes to the simulation of synthetic attitudes towards political affairs and elections (e.g. Argyle et al . 2023 ; Bisbee et al . 2024 ). In short, we provide an LLM-powered conversational agent with a context, and then ask the agent to provide a response to a close-ended survey questionnaire, and to enclose the response in a .csv format. Because LLM responses converge relatively quickly, we follow conventions and generate 30 synthetic responses for each real human respondent in the true survey ( Bisbee et al

. 2024 ), yielding a final data set of 277,470 ( 30 × 9249 30\times 9249 ) responses. 3.3. Key measures 3.3.1. Music tastes We operationalize taste using a set of items from the SPPA. Respondents are asked to provide a binary yes/no response to the question, “Do you like to listen to {music genre}?” Seventeen music genres are admitted into consideration: (1) classical or chamber music, (2) opera, (3) Broadway musicals or show tunes, (4) jazz, (5) blues or rhythm and blues, (6) gospel or other religious music, (7) country music, (8) bluegrass, (9) folk music, (10) classic rock, (11) alternative or independent rock, (12) pop music, (13) rap or hip-hop, (14) electronic or dance music, (15) reggae, (16) Latin or salsa music, and (17) Asian, African, or Middle-Eastern music. We treat each affirmative response as an expression of taste for that genre. For each respondent i i , we collect these affirmative responses into a taste set t i t_{i} , defined as the subset of the seventeen genres for which respondent i i reports a liking. This set-valued representation of taste serves as the basic unit for the distance measures introduced below, while the genre-level binary indicators serve as the outcomes in the linear probability models described in Section 4.3 . 3.3.2. Conclusion Silicon sampling promises cheap, scalable substitutes for human survey respondents. This paper asks how far that promise extends to cultural taste—a domain where preferences are structured not only by aggregate levels of liking but by the relational organization of genres and their alignment with social location. Using 277,470 silicon surrogates from OpenAI, Anthropic, and DeepSeek, matched to respondents from the Survey of Public Participation in the Arts , we find that LLM-generated tastes are highly stylized facsimiles of human tastes: plausible at the surface, but systematically wrong in ways that would mislead researchers who reify them as interchangeable with flesh-and-blood data. In this study, we assessed the fidelity of silicon samples in three major ways, by considering (a) their ecological fidelity, (b) their relationality fidelity, and (c) how closely they are able to reproduce known multivariate associations between tastes and social location At the ecological level, silicon samples over-like . Silicon respondents have a pervasive positive bias to their music tastes, so much so that they appear to be hyper-omnivores when compa

red to their SPPA counterparts. This is particularly so when it comes to less popular genres of music. This bias cannot simply be explained by the minority penalty – in many cases, silicon samples’ biases were greater among wealthier, more educated, and majority-race (white) populations. Silicon tastes do not preserve such the relationality among tastes faithfully. Human tastes have a rich and well-understood relational structure: certain tastes (e.g. preferences for classical music) “go-with” some (e.g. preferences for opera) more than others (e.g. preferences for country music). While silicon samples have an ersatz similarity in terms of aggregate associations, we find the underlying relational structure of silicon tastes to be poorly aligned to human ones. Strong between-genre associations, such as that of blues and classic rock, are completely attenuated among silicon samples. Further, these discrepancies in relationality fidelity are larger among the less-popular genres. Finally, silicon tastes do not share typically-human associations with social parts of social space. Silicon samples over-associate music tastes with the young; they resurrect retrograde class-taste homologies; they caricaturize gender-taste associations; they invent fictive race-taste associations.
