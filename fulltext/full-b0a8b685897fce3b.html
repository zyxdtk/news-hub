The extent to which LLMs are able to produce reasonable approximations of cultural taste remains an open empirical question that becomes more urgent by the day, with market research companies already offering provisional ‘synthetic’ survey panels and the contamination of standard survey data from LLM-generated responses.

Assessing the fidelity of such silicon tastes is crucial for two main reasons.
First, silicon tastes, if faithful, allow for research designs formerly infeasible.
Silicon sampling make a seductive promise to researchers.
LLMs answer surveys quickly, cheaply, and without fatigue or complaint.
It may now be possible to cheaply and quickly produce surveys of public opinion; to field surveys that were thought to be too long, laborious, or boring; to effectively go back in time and ask and then re-ask the questions that you couldn’t or didn’t think so.
For social scientists interested in the study of tastes, silicon samples could facilitate finer-grain investigations than previously possible.
Pr

esent surveys of cultural taste tend to rely on responses to fuzzy cultural categories, such as genres.
As Vandebroeck (
Vandebroeck

Genres, in other words, are inherently fuzzy, and it is far from obvious that respondents share the taxonomy a survey imposes on them.
Yet fielding surveys that probe finer aspects of cultural taste is difficult, since this makes a survey long, tedious, and expensive.
Were silicon samples to prove faithful, they would let researchers interrogate taste at the level of specific works, performances, or sub-genres, and to vary the framing of cultural categories experimentally, at a scale and cost that fielding such instruments on human respondents could never match.
Further, silicon samples would facilitate interpolated projections of cultural taste in time and space, allowing up to improve our understanding of the ecology of tastes over time.
Extrapolation of public opinion surveys into social space with limited support is a dangerous proposition, but interpolation over known spaces may be plausible and rewarding in and of itself.
There are no notable panel studies of cultural taste, and few repeated cross-sectional studies of cultural taste (
Ma
2020
).
Silicon sampling may al

Assessing the fidelity of silicon sampling is pressing because the practice is expanding rapidly—in the range of domains to which it is applied, in the sophistication of its methods, and in its rapid expansion by survey and market research companies.
Researchers have used LLMs to simulate responses to contentious political items drawn from the American National Election Studies (

2.
Literature review
2.1.
Silicon sampling and the study of taste
Large language models (LLMs) are probabilistic machine learning models that are used to process and produce textual data.
At a high level, LLMs resemble the autocomplete technologies that have be

come on search engines and other digital spaces, but with considerably greater scale and sophistication (
Bail
2024
).
They power the dialogue agents that have already experienced widespread consumer adoption.
In this study, we extend ongoing work that applies LLMs to survey research.
In particular, we focus on the use of LLMs to simulate synthetic human samples in public opinion surveys, a practice often referred to as
silicon sampling
.
The intuition behind silicon sampling is simple. Homo silicus has advanced to such a state that many LLMs are able to converse and reason like humans, that the prospect of AI passing Turing tests just about seems a fait accompli (
Dillion et al
.
2023
).
If homo silicus really was capable of providing such a great facsimile of human responses, then it may be possible to productively sample responses from LLMs, the same way we would from human populations.
LLMs by the nature of their training and design are computational models of humans and likely possess a great deal of latent social information (
Horton
2023
).
This latent information can often evince itself negatively in different guises of algorithmic bias, Argyle et al. (
(
2023
)
) argue that it may be possible to instead think of them in productive terms as a model of the “complex reflection of the many various patterns of association between ideas, attitudes, and contexts present among humans.”
The algorithmic fidelity of LLMs, when appropriately conditioned, means that researchers m

ay be able to induce the model to produce outputs that correlate with the attitudes, opinions, and experiences of distinct human subpopulations.
As George Box put it, while all simplified models of human behavior are necessarily wrong, some are useful.
So it may be the case for silicon samples of human respondents.
Work is also underway exploring the applications of synthetic surrogates of human respondents in domains beyond survey research, from agent-based modeling, to psychological experiments, to behavioral economics experiments, to market research (
Chen et al
.
2023
; Dillion et al
.
2023
; Horton
2023
; Park et al
.
2023
; Goli and Singh
2024
).
In our study, we advance ongoing work on the robustness and bounds of silicon sampling by applying to cultural fields, in particular exploring the application of silicon sampling to the case of public opinion surveys of cultural taste.
Taste in its most literal form refers to gustatory taste, our perception of sweet, salty, bitter, sour, and savory sensations as mediated by a physiologically-defined chemosensory system.
From the 16th century and on, however, taste acquired a separate metaphorical meaning as an expression of a person’s aesthetic orientation towards culture, that is, cultural taste.
Cultural taste is commonly operationalized as a person’s first-order preferences, feelings, or desires towards an object in a cultural field (
Ma
2024
).
We use the term “silicon tastes” to refer to the tastes and preferences expresse

d by silicon surrogates of human respondents.
2.2.
Assessing the fidelity of silicon tastes
How
faithful are silicon tastes to their carbon counterparts?
We contribute to ongoing work examing the bounds and robustness of silicon sampling by examing the quality and fidelity of synthetic cultural tastes.
We assess (1) the ecological fidelity of silicon tastes, and whether such validity is mediated by the so-called WEIRD bias of LLMs, (2) the higher-order relations among silicon tastes, and (3) the fidelity of taste-to-social-space associations inferred from silicon samples.
We ask three main research questions.
(1)
How faithful are the ecological estimates of cultural tastes produced by silicon samples? And to what extent are discrepancies in estimates explained by the WEIRD-bias?
(2)
To what extent do silicon samples preserve the relationality among tastes?
(3)
To what extent are known cultural associations between tastes and social space preserved among silicon samples?
To answer the first question, we compare the ecological estimates of cultural tastes for 17 genres of music from real and silicon samples.
Then, we compute and model the Jaccard distance between the real and silicon tastes.
To answer the second, we examine and compare the correlation matrices of real and silicon tastes.
To answer the third, we model the coefficient bias of silicon samples through a mixed-effects meta-regression.

Data source
The SPPA is a repeated cross-sectional survey that measures cultural attitudes and participation in the United States.

5.
Conclusion
Silicon sampling promises cheap, scalable substitutes for human survey respondents.
This paper asks how far that promise extends to cultural taste—a domain where preferences are structured not only by aggregate levels of liking but by the relational organization of genres and their alignment with social location.
Using 277,470 silicon surrogates from OpenAI, Anthropic, and DeepSeek, matched to respondents from the
Survey of Public Participation in the Arts
, we find that LLM-generated tastes are highly stylized facsimiles of human tastes: plausible at the surface, but systematically wrong in ways that would mislead researchers who reify them as interchangeable with flesh-and-blood data.
In this study, we assessed the fidelity of silicon samples in three major ways, by considering (a) their ecological fidelity, (b) their relationality fidelity, and (c) how closely they are able to reproduce known multivariate associations between tastes and social location
At the ecological level, silicon samples
over-like
.
Silicon respondents have a pervasive positive bias to their music tastes, so much so that they appear to be hyper-omnivores when compa

red to their SPPA counterparts.
This is particularly so when it comes to less popular genres of music.
This bias cannot simply be explained by the minority penalty – in many cases, silicon samples’ biases were greater among wealthier, more educated, and majority-race (white) populations.
Silicon tastes do not preserve such the relationality among tastes faithfully.
Human tastes have a rich and well-understood relational structure: certain tastes (e.g. preferences for classical music) “go-with” some (e.g. preferences for opera) more than others (e.g. preferences for country music).
While silicon samples have an ersatz similarity in terms of aggregate associations, we find the underlying relational structure of silicon tastes to be poorly aligned to human ones.
Strong between-genre associations, such as that of blues and classic rock, are completely attenuated among silicon samples.
Further, these discrepancies in relationality fidelity are larger among the less-popular genres.
Finally, silicon tastes do not share typically-human associations with social parts of social space.
Silicon samples over-associate music tastes with the young; they resurrect retrograde class-taste homologies; they caricaturize gender-taste associations; they invent fictive race-taste associations.
