Abstract This paper introduces a new paradigm for AI game programming, leveraging large language models (LLMs) to extend and operationalize Claude Shannon’s taxonomy of game-playing machines. Central to this paradigm is Nemobot , an interactive agentic engineering environment that enables users to create, customize, and deploy LLM-powered game agents while actively engaging with AI-driven strategies. The LLM-based chatbot, integrated within Nemobot, demonstrates its capabilities across four distinct classes of games. For dictionary-based games , it compresses state-action mappings into efficient, generalized models for rapid adaptability. In rigorously solvable games , it employs mathematical reasoning to compute optimal strategies and generates human-readable explanations for its decisions. For heuristic-based games , it synthesizes strategies by combining insights from classical minimax algorithms (see, e.g., [ 1 ] ) with crowd-sourced data. Finally, in learning-based games , it utilizes reinforcement learning with human feedback and self-critique to iteratively refine strategies through trial-and-error and imitation learning. lifies this framework by offering a programmable environment where users can experiment with tool-augmented generation and fine-tuning of strategic game agents. From strategic games to role-playing games, Nemobot demonstrates how AI agents can achieve a form of self-programming by integrating crowdsourced learning and human creativity to iteratively refine their own logic. This represents a step toward the long-term goal of self-programming AI. I Introduction Claude Shannon, a pioneer in artificial intelligence, posed a thought-provoking question in his seminal work [ 2 ] : “Can we program a digital computer so that (eventually) 99 percent of the orders it follows are written by the computer itself, rather than the few percent in current programs?” This question encapsulates the vision of self-programming systems, a concept that remains a core challenge in AI research. Similarly, Marvin Minsky argued in [ 3 ] that programming’s structured rules are not limitations but tools for refining and clarifying poorly understood ideas, akin to how artists and composers use formal constraints to express creativity. Shannon’s vision of computers autonomously generating their own instructions has become increasingly relevant in agentic engineering, where large language models (LLMs) now produce vast amounts of code, driving self-evolving AI systems and interactive applications such as games. II-B Programmable Prompts via LLM Functions Large language models have the ability to synthesize knowledge from massive (raw and unlabelled) datasets, making them powerful tools for autonomous learning and decision-making [ 18 ] . We can design LLM agents that do not merely follow pre-programmed rules but can generate strategies, responses, and solutions based on the context they encounter. This approach aligns with Shannon’s vision of a machine that gradually writes more of its own orders. For example, in game-playing systems, instead of being limited to predefined strategies or rigid algorithms, a query can be made to different LLMs to generate feasible strategies to improve game learning performance iteratively with minimal human input. Programmable prompt engineering further enhances this capacity by allowing developers to implement logical response behavior using LLM functions . Rather than writing exhaustive code to cover every possible situation, prompt engineering allows the program to dynamically understand and respond

to varied situations through contextually relevant instructions in real-time. This allows for a flexible, scalable approach to problem-solving where the machine “writes” its own orders through prompt-based guidance. LLM functions thus serve to generate strategic decisions in games, adapt its behavior based on previous outcomes, and refine its strategies through feedback loops. Hence, instead of relying solely on pre-programmed strategies, an AI game engine can autonomously generate new strategies by interpreting prompts and adjusting its play based on real-time feedback. This shift toward greater autonomy via machine learning for conversational data (see, e.g., [ 40 ] ) is not only a recent technical achievement but also reflects a deeper philosophical transition in how we interact with machines, thus moving from instructing them directly to guiding them in a way that allows them to “write” their own programs via natural language generation and understanding. Michie’s contemporaneous “memo functions” or memoization in [ 41 , 42 , 37 ] are now understood as a form of speedup learning that stores and reuses previously-computed results, reducing redundancy and enhancing efficiency. Hence, LLM functions can be seen as a form of neuralized memoization , offering more dynamic, context-aware responses. III-B System Architecture and Implementation The Nemobot system architecture is designed for modularity, low latency, and scalability. The system comprises three core layers: • Model-Agnostic AI Integration: The platform is architected to be independent of any specific Large Language Model (LLM). It operates on a “Bring Your Own Key” (BYOK) principle, allowing users to connect via API to various models (e.g., GPT-4, GPT-3.5) depending on their performance requirements and budget. This ensures the framework remains adaptable as model capabilities evolve. • State Management and Latency: To ensure a responsive ga

meplay experience, the game logic and state management are handled locally within the execution environment. During active gameplay, game states are stored in-memory to minimize latency. The LLM is queried only when the user explicitly triggers an “LLM function” for strategy construction or reasoning, ensuring that the core game loop remains fast and is not bottlenecked by inference latency. • Data Persistence and Crowdsourcing: While individual game states are transient, performance metrics and training outcomes are batched and asynchronously synchronized with a cloud-based database. This allows for the aggregation of crowdsourced data (e.g., win/loss ratios, successful heuristics) across the user base without disrupting real-time interactions, facilitating the collaborative learning described in Section II . III-D Tiered Curriculum Nemobot supports developers with varying expertise by offering structured pathways for crafting AI agents, inspired by Claude E. Shannon’s classifications of game-playing machines [ 2 ] : dictionary-type machines, complete analysis machines, approximate principle machines, and learning machines. These pathways guide developers from fundamental heuristic design to advanced learning-based AI implementations: • Foundational Development: Developers begin by constructing dictionary-type AI agents for predefined games, defining actions a i a_{i} for each state s i s_{i} . This provides insight into how decision-making heuristics influence gameplay outcomes. • Intermediate Rule-Based Systems: Using NodeJS templates, developers create custom games and build rule-based AI that follows decision algorithms f ​ ( a i | s i ) f(a_{i}|s_{i}) . Heuristics are iteratively refined based on testing data, introducing the principles of optimization. • Advanced Data-Driven AI: Developers employ LLM functions to design complex, adaptive AI systems. By leveraging automated testing methods and crowdsourced training data, they transition to building learning-based AI capable of self-improvement. This structured progression focuses on heuristic-driven decision-making as the foundation and gradually incorporates data-driven methodologies. In the first prong, simulations employ Donald Michie’s Boxes algorithm through self-play. Moves are chosen probabilistically based on the current state’s “box”; winning moves increase the probability of reselection, while losing moves decrease it. Over iterations, this trial-and-error process uncovers optimal strategies across diverse scenarios without requiring expert examples. The second prong involves crowdsourcing, where human players contribute diverse moves by playing against an agent using Shannon’s classical minimax algorithm [ 1 ] . This exposes the LLM agent to a broader range of tactics and strategies, improving its adaptability to different gameplay styles. Additionally, the LLM agent acts as an instructor, analyzing gameplay, offering real-time feedback on mistakes, and suggesting optimal strategies. This accelerates learning by highlighting suboptimal moves and guiding exploration toward better alternatives, fostering more strategic, long-term decision-making. V Further Discussions V-A Implications and Positioning in the Literature To situate this work within the broader landscape of LLM-based game AI, it is instructive to compare Nemobot with several prominent systems. Voyager [ 48 ] is an open-ended embodied agent that uses GPT-4 to explore and acquire skills autonomously in Minecraft; however, it operates as a fully autonomous agent with no mechanism for human programmers to inspect, modify, or share the strategies it discovers. ChessGPT [ 8 ] bridges policy learning and language modeling for chess, yet it is specialized to a single domain and does not expose a programmable interface for educational use. Generative Agents [ 49 ] simulate believable human behavior through LLM-driven memory and reflection, but their focus is on social simulation rather than strategic game programming. Suspicion-Agent [ 10 ] integrates theory-of-mind reasoning with GPT-4 for imperfect-information games, demonstrating sophisticated multi-agent interactions but without an educational or programming-oriented framework. In contrast, Nemobot occupies a distinct niche: it is a programmable, educational framework that exposes LLM functions as transpar

ent, modular components within Shannon’s taxonomy. Users do not merely consume AI-generated gameplay; they actively design, test, and refine game-playing strategies through collaborative prompt engineering and crowdsourced feedback. This programmability distinguishes Nemobot from systems that treat LLMs as black-box players and aligns with the broader vision of AI-assisted programming [ 31 , 26 , 21 ] where human oversight and iterative refinement are central to the development process. By grounding its design in Shannon’s classical framework and extending it with modern LLM capabilities, Nemobot bridges foundational AI theory with contemporary practice, offering both research value and educational utility. Finally, the algorithmic framework in this paper extends naturally beyond game playing to knowledge tracing and learning-to-learn in educational AI [ 50 ] . In knowledge tracing, a trial-and-error mechanism (e.g., Michie’s Boxes algorithm) can model a student’s evolving mastery of concepts: each knowledge component becomes a “box,” the student’s response (correct or incorrect) serves as reward or punishment, and the probability of selecting a particular strategy updates accordingly–enabling real-time prediction of future performance without requiring expert-labeled skill models. Crowdsourcing, meanwhile, aligns with learning-to-learn by aggregating solution strategies from diverse learners, allowing an intelligent tutoring system to meta-learn which pedagogical approaches

Future research could explore hierarchical systems inspired by Shannon’s hierarchy [ 2 ] , where learning advances through abstraction. At the highest level, programming copilots could leverage predicate logic for advanced planning, using inductive logic programming to derive predicates from examples and background knowledge [ 52 ] . • Another area of exploration involves balancing generalization and specialization. While LLMs are trained on vast datasets to generalize effectively, applying them to specific game domains often requires extensive fine-tuning. Developing adaptive fine-tuning methods that allow for real-time adjustments based on gameplay feedback could enable LLMs to transition seamlessly between different game types or environments. • Additionally, the reliance on human feedback for fine-tuning poses scalability challenges. Although crowdsourced feedback can accelerate learning, ensuring the quality and consistency of this input remains a bottleneck. Future work could explore automated feedback systems or hybrid approaches combining human oversight with AI-generated evaluations. • Recent work on adversarial bots [ 53 , 54 ] shows that, while capable of defeating top Go-playing AI (e.g., AlphaGo [ 9 ] ), they are trained to exploit hidden vulnerabilities rather than develop well-rounded strategies. A challenge is thus to balance competitive performance with ethical desi

gn. Developers must ensure bots engage fairly and transparently, avoid exploiting human weaknesses, and foster challenging yet enjoyable gameplay. Achieving this balance with big data is crucial as AI systems advance, raising important ethical considerations for human-AI interactions in gaming [ 55 ] . • Striking a balance between computational efficiency and reproducibility is a critical challenge, particularly for real-time interactive games that leverage big data. LLMs are highly resource-intensive, making their deployment at scale computationally expensive. To address this, research into lightweight, efficient LLM architectures and hybrid systems is crucial. These systems could offload less complex tasks to smaller, more efficient models while reserving the full capabilities of LLMs for tasks that demand greater sophistication. By achieving this balance, it becomes possible to not only enhance scalability and reduce costs but also ensure reproducibility by enabling consistent benchmarks for performance, resource utilization, and response accuracy across varied applications [ 55 ] . Addressing these challenges will not only improve AI game systems but also advance broader applications of LLMs in autonomous decision-making and interactive learning.
