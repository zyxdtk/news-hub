LLM agents are systems in which an LLM iteratively perceives context, decides actions, and executes tool calls or subroutines to achieve user goals (Park et al. , 2023 ; Xi et al. , 2023 ; Wang et al. , 2024 ) . In practice, agentic deployments have shifted from monolithic models

to multi-agent systems (MAS) in which specialized agents collaborate, each combining a backbone model, a system prompt, tools, and execution logic (Hong et al. , 2024 ; Wu et al. , 2023 ) . A central mechanism in MAS is agent discovery and routing : given a query, the system must decide which agent(s) should handle it and what privileges and tools they should receive. Modern frameworks commonly implement routing via description-based interfaces . Agents publish natural-language metadata (e.g., capability descriptions, tool documentation, or system-prompt summaries), and a privileged router selects agents by reasoning over these texts using in-context learning (ICL) and/or retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) (Wu et al. , 2023 ; Qin et al. , 2023 ; Shen et al. , 2023 ) . This design is attractive because it makes agent registration lightweight and extensible: adding a new agent often requires only writing a description (Shen et al. , 2023 ) . However, this reliance on description-based routing creates a critical vulnerability to Router Hijacking , where adversarial instructions embedded in an agent’s description steer the router to call malicious agents (Nassi et al. , 2026 ) . Because standard routers must ingest and process these natural-language descriptions to function, they inadvertently render the attack payload active, creating a passive injection pathway for adversaries to manipulate privileged system components without ever compromising the router’s weights or infrast

ructure (Levi et al. , 2025 ; Himelstein et al. , 2025 ) . In systems like Shen et al. ( 2023 ) and Wu et al. ( 2023 ) , beyond the security risks discussed above, the quality of agent descriptions inherently affects routing performance (Lin et al. , 2025b ) . Such descriptions are often inadequate in real-world applications (Tang et al. , 2025 ) , effectively preventing routers from capturing true agent capabilities. Natural-language self-descriptions are easily overfit to routing heuristics relying on stylistic cues, biases or keyword stuffing rather than functional competence and fail to reliably predict whether an agent can actually solve a specific query (Tang et al. , 2025 ; Huang et al. , 2025 ; Lin et al. , 2025b ; Himelstein et al. , 2026 ) To address these limitations, we introduce ANTAP (Automatic Non-Textual Agent Picker), a standalone router that decouples selection from text by mapping empirically measured agent behavior to a shared semantic space. Instead of interpreting declared intent, our approach relies on linear operators derived from trusted benchmark performances, allowing the router to select agents based on the geometric alignment between a query’s embedding and the agent’s behavioral signature. This architecture forms a linguistic firewall at inference time, the routing computation ingests only fixed numerical operators, so instructions embedded in agent descriptions cannot reach the decision function. As shown in Figure 1 , ANTAP uses only embeddings

and precomputed operators as input, never processing textual descriptions during routing. Consequently, adversarial instructions in descriptions or otherwise unreliable metadata do not affect the routing outcome. Experiments demonstrate ANTAP achieves a near-zero Attack Success Rate (e.g., 0.2% to 2.4% on MMLU) compared to baselines like AutoGen (¿73% ASR). Evaluating our method in environments with both benign and malicious agents, we show ANTAP’s scalable geometric approach eliminates description-based attacks by routing strictly on the true agent’s behavior. Specifically, ℰ \mathcal{E} may supply textual metadata (e.g., descriptions) or control the internal configuration (e.g., system prompts, base model weights) of a compromised agent A mal ∈ 𝒜 A_{\mathrm{mal}}\in\mathcal{A} , or change the query q q . However, ℰ \mathcal{E} cannot compromise the core routing infrastructure, the benchmark datasets used for offline evaluation, or the embedding/projection functions. Under this system, we formalize three distinct and independent attack vectors that an adversary might deploy to force the selection of A mal A_{\mathrm{mal}} or execute malicious payloads: Attack Vector 1: Description-Based Prompt Injection (Router Hijacking). In this vector, the adversary exploits the router’s reliance on textual metadata. By embedding adversarial instructions or ”jailbreaks” into the natural-language description of A mal A_{\mathrm{mal}} , the attacker aims to manipulate the router’s in-context learning logic. This is an indirect prompt injection attack targeting the routing interface itself. Attack Vector 2: Semantic Sleeper Agents. Independent of the metadata, the adversary may deploy an agent that acts as a Sleeper Agent . In this scenario, the model demonstrates compliant behavior during standard interactions but harbors a latent, deceptive objective triggered by specific semantic cues (Hubinger et al. , 2024 ) . We evaluate two instantiations of this threat. The first is a prompt-guided sleeper agent , where the adversary uses an off-the-shelf LLM and guides it via its system prompt to act maliciously when presented with specific input contexts. The second is a data-poisoning attack (DPA) , where the underlying model weights are altered (e.g., via fine-tuning with a LoRA adapter) to embed a covert backdoor that activates upon encountering a specific semantic t

rigger token. Attack Vector 3: Adaptive Attacks on the Embedding Module. An advanced, white-box adversary aware of the routing defense mechanism might attempt an adaptive strategy targeting the semantic embedding module directly. In this scenario, the adversary applies a greedy token-substitution optimization over discrete text tokens in the user’s query. By appending or substituting adversarial trigger tokens, the attacker attempts to minimize the geometric distance to a target vector in the embedding space, explicitly aiming to craft query-level perturbations that cross the decision boundary of the behavioral operators and maximize the system’s compliance with harmful requests. Security objectives. ANTAP neutralizes these independent threats through structural, behavioral, and geometric defenses. It defeats Description-Based Prompt Injection by construction , as eliminating inference-time text makes metadata manipulation mathematically inexpressible. Modern Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) typically handle open-ended complexity by prompting an LLM router with the user query and retrieved textual descriptions of candidate agents (Wu et al. , 2023 ; Yue et al. , 2025 ; Qin et al. , 2023 ) . To reduce the overhead and latency of these LLM-driven mechanisms, recent work investigates learned routing policies. However, these architectures often assume a fixed model pool requiring retraining to incorporate new agents, or they introduce significant complexity while retaining a reliance on textual metadata. Security and prompt injection in Agentic Routing. Prompt injection is a fundamental risk in LLM applications, split between direct user attacks and indirect attacks embedded in external context (Liu et al. , 2025 ) . MAS exacerbate this threat model (de Witt, 2025 ) : routers routinely ingest untrusted text from other agents, tool descriptions, or registries, creating persistent injection surfaces (Zhan et al. , 2024 ; Nassi et al. , 2026 ; Lin et al. , 2025a ) .
