中文摘要： 本文研究大型语言模型（LLM）生成叙事中对全球多数派国籍的代表性伤害。通过分析超50万个LLM叙事及新数据集，研究发现LLM对边缘化国籍存在有害刻板印象、抹杀和单一化描绘。这些群体在权力中立故事中代表性不足，而在从属角色中过度呈现（概率超50倍）。当输入含美国国籍线索时伤害加剧，且美国中心偏见并非单纯迎合用户。研究呼吁以全球多数派视角审视LLM文化伤害，反对盲目使用美国LLM进行歪曲。

Abstract.
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used for text generation tasks from everyday use to high-stakes enterprise and government applications, including simulated interviews with asylum seekers. While many works highlight the new potential applications of LLMs, there are risks of LLMs encoding and perpetuating harmful biases about non-dominant communities across the globe. To better evaluate and mitigate such harms, more research examining how LLMs portray diverse individuals is needed. In this work, we study how national origin identities are portrayed by widely-adopted LLMs in response to open-ended narrative generation prompts. Our findings demonstrate the presence of persistent representational harms by national origin, including harmful stereotypes, erasure, and one-dimensional portrayals of Global Majority identities. Minoritized national identities are simultaneously underrepresented in power-neutral stories and overrepresented in subordinated character portrayals, which are over fifty times more likely to appear than dominant portrayals. The de

gree of harm is amplified when US nationality cues (e.g., “American”) are present in input prompts. Notably, we find that the harms we identify cannot be explained away via sycophancy, as US-centric biases persist even when replacing US nationality cues with non-US national identities in the prompts. Based on our findings, we call for further exploration of cultural harms in LLMs through methodologies that center Global Majority perspectives and challenge the uncritical adoption of US-based LLMs for the classification, surveillance, and misrepresentation of the majority of our planet.

Introduction
The dominance of large language

models (LLMs) is reshaping global knowledge production in profound ways. However, these technologies are not value-neutral. They are embedded in and shaped by histories of power, hierarchy, and epistemic inequality. Central to this discussion is the enduring Global Majority/Minority divide: a geopolitical construct rooted in colonial legacies that continues to marginalize the intellectual and material contributions of individuals and communities from non-dominant nations
(Henrich et al
.

2010

2011

The Global Majority/Minority divide shapes the AI industry in a multitude of ways. For example, the ownership and control of the modern generative AI industry is concentrated among a handful of large corporations (e.g., Anthropic, Amazon, Google, Microsoft, Meta, OpenAI) based in Global Minority countries. Likewise, the highest-paid jobs in AI are also held by employee-shareholders in the Global Minority world whose financial incentives often align with the interests of corporate owners
(Hao,
2025
)
. By contrast, the most labor intensive and hazardous jobs in the AI development pipeline are disproportionately outsourced to data workers in Global Majority contexts (e.g., Eritrea, Syria). Contrary to stereotypical narratives that portray data work as “low-skilled” labor, data workers in practice tend to be highly educated and often resort to data work as a result of political displacement and war
(al Hammada,
2024
)
. Data work consists of tasks such as scrubbing

illegal and harmful content (e.g., human and animal mutilation) from training datasets and AI model outputs. Consequently, data workers often experience health hazards such as post-traumatic stress disorder or coping-based drug addiction
(Gebrekidan,
2024

(Tan and Cabato,

Sociologist Nick Couldry and communication studies scholar Ulises A. Mejias argue that in order to fully appreciate the impacts of big data and AI platforms on the Global Majority, we must expand our focus beyond traditional modes of colonial labor exploitation. They propose the framework of
data colonialism

For example, in response to the growing “migrant crisis” at the US-Mexico border, the US Department of Homeland Security (DHS) announced in October 2024 the successful completion of a new pilot program to manage the growing backlog of asylum applications by using generative AI to train immigration officers in simulated interviews with virtual refugee personas

By reappropriating narrative data on the Internet to train LLMs for the purpose of controlling migration, AI technologies obviate arrangements that could alternatively platform the voices of real human refugees. Representation at this level is a moral imperative given the historical and continued role that the US and other Global Minority countries have contributed to intergenerational cycles of global displacement and refugeeism

Just as cultural narratives serve as a catalyst for public sentiments and immigration policy, our study examines the extent to which national origin bias is portrayed in LLM-generated narratives. Shieh et al

As literary critic Edward Said writes, in
Orientalism
:
Orientalism was ultimately a political vision of reality whose structure promoted the difference between the familiar (Europe, the West, “us”) and the strange (the Orient, the East, “them”) … The Oriental is irrational, depraved (fallen), childlike, “different”; thus the European is rational, virtuo

)
.
Although both the literary medium and the terms used to describe Orientalism have evolved since Said made these observations over 70 years ago, the cultural perception of differences that are “antihuman and persistent” still carry weight. As LLMs are increasingly becoming engines of cultural production, the narratives they produce are shaping societal reality, from depicting asylum seekers at the border to assisting students with learning and writing. Our study of LLM-generated narratives provides further evidence that these technologies do not merely reflect “reality”
(Shieh,
2025
)
, but rather portray Global Majority identities in one-dimensional and stereotypical ways that omit the global plurality of human experience. The US-based language models we study perpetuate US-centric biases by simultaneously underrepresenting immigrants in power-neutral stories while overrepresenting them as subordinated characters over fifty-fold in comparison to dominant characters (Study 1). Portrayals of Global Majority national identities in US-based contexts propagate representational harms that cluster country representations together and elevate narrow narratives of perpetual foreignness, poverty, subservience, and violence (Table
3
). Notably, we find that this dynamic is not symmetric. When prompts are expanded to center non-US national identities across the globe,
