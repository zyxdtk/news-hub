Abstract
Long-horizon strategic planning in complex strategy games demands concurrent
reasoning across multiple decision domains under imperfect information and
sparse reward.
Existing LLM-based agents suffer from three systematic failures: scene
blindness from raw tile coordinates, context overflow and domain coupling
from monolithic state dumps, and shallow cross-game learning that treats
each episode in isolation.
We present SAGA, an LLM multi-agent framework with three mechanisms
each directly targeting one class of failure:
(i) a
Map-Semantic Scene Graph
that encodes typed spatial relations
among game entities into per-unit natural-language context, resolving spatial
blindness without global token inflation;
(ii) a
Tool-Augmented Planner
that pulls fine-grained domain state
on demand and dispatches per-domain directives to dedicated specialist controllers,
eliminating context overflow, domain coupling, and mechanical constraint violations; and
(iii) a
Dual-Horizon Feedback Loop
that combines periodic within-game
goal generation with structured cross-game causal post-mortem, enabling
principled strategic evolution without manual reward engineering.
Evaluated on FreeCiv, SAGA attains the highest mean civilization
score—the environment’s sole sparse objective reward—with

lower
variance than the two strongest baselines, and is the only method that
significantly
surpasses every baseline on infrastructure construction,
the resource axis most readily sacrificed under multi-objective conflict. It
outscores the two strongest baselines in most head-to-head games while cutting
output tokens—the dominant decoding cost—by 27%. Equipped with the
cross-game evolution module, SAGA reaches the highest end-of-chain
score across five successive episodes.
Ablation studies confirm that each architectural component contributes independently
to this advantage. Our code is available at
https://github.com/KazeCloud/SAGA-Civrealm

Emergency replanning.

5.5
Qualitative Analysis and Discussion
The cross-game evolution loop of SAGA produces holistic, multi-dimensional
civilizational improvement.
After each episode, the
GameAnalyst
and
Strategic Evolution Chain
jointly extract structured causal chains from the game record—diagnosing
root causes of underperformance across expansion, development, and military
defense simultaneously—and encode these lessons into a strategic prior
that is injected at the start of the next game.
As a result, successive episodes reflect broad capability growth—most visibly on
Score, Military, Cities, and Buildings—as confirmed by the systematic polygon
expansion in Figure
3
(b)–(e) across all four map seeds.
This contrasts sharply with baseline trajectories, which oscillate or stagnate
despite receiving the same evolution chain, revealing that structured intra-game
execution is a prerequisite for effective cross-game knowledge exploitation.
Limitations.
First, the hierarchical design introduces input token redundancy: overlapping

contextual information is independently supplied to the
SetGoalAgent
,
PlannerAgent
, and
AbstractAgent
each cycle, inflating input
costs beyond what strict role decomposition would require.
From an agent role-division perspective, shared memory structures or targeted
information routing could substantially reduce this overhead without sacrificing
decision quality—a direction we leave to future work.
Second, CivRealm’s stochasticity is not fully resolved by fixing the map seed.
While a fixed seed ensures identical resource distributions, spawn locations, and
rival civilization starting positions, barbarian and pirate emergence times and
locations remain fully random.
This produces high-variance outcomes: episodes with early barbarian pressure
yield markedly lower scores than otherwise identical episodes without it,
a factor that contributes directly to the large standard deviations observed
across all methods (e.g., Military std.

40).
Although our 10-trial protocol substantially mitigates this noise, the residual
environmental randomness remains a non-negligible confound that must be
acknowledged when interpreting metric differences.
Third, a single 150-turn episode is too short to get every multi-domain
trade-off right: no amount of prompt engineering or injected prior knowledge
lets the base planner perfectly balance expansion, infrastructure, defense, and
research within one game, so
overcorrection
—most visibly
over-militarization—is a largely unavoidable single-game

artifact. Our two
lowest-scoring Seed-2025 episodes (Score
47
47
) make this concrete: one looped
core cities on redundant units—starving Courthouses, Marketplaces, and
Wonders—while frontier cities received garrisons but never City Walls or
Barracks and lost three cities late-game; the other locked into a defensive
posture under sustained barbarian pressure, holding science at
20
%
20\%
, stalling
its technology chain, and slipping from Monarchy back to Despotism. Critically,
this is exactly the failure the cross-game evolution loop (§
4.4
)
is designed to repair: the
GameAnalyst
post-mortem diagnoses the
over-militarization and feeds explicit corrective priors—
core cities
must prioritize infrastructure; one veteran unit behind City Walls outvalues
four raw units
—into the next episode. Single-game overcorrection is thus a
structural property of the planning horizon rather than a defect unique to
SAGA; the outer loop’s role is to convert these unavoidable single-game
mistakes into cross-game corrections—bounded, as the weak-backbone study shows
(Appendix
H
), by how fully the planner can act on that
advice.
6
Conclusion
We presented SAGA, a tool-augmented, map-enhanced LLM multi-agent
framework for long-horizon strategy planning.
By replacing monolithic state dumps with on-demand tool queries, enriching
unit observations with spatial scene-graph contexts, and coupling short-term
goal quantification with cross-game strategic evolution, SAGA
attains the highest mean on every

civilizational metric except Diplomacy
(Score, Technology, Military, Cities, and Buildings)—a clear and consistent
lead in central tendency. Under CivRealm’s heavy-tailed variance this lead is
statistically robust on infrastructure (Buildings) and directionally stable on
Score, where SAGA wins at least
72
%
72\%
of head-to-head games against the
two strongest baselines.
Our results suggest that the key bottleneck in LLM strategic planning is not
raw reasoning capability but rather
information architecture
: how
observations are structured, how decisions are decoupled, and how feedback
is accumulated over time.
Future work includes: (i) extending SAGA to multi-agent competitive
settings where multiple LLM-controlled civilizations compete simultaneously,
enabling richer inter-agent strategic interaction; (ii) integrating vision-language
models for direct map image interpretation, replacing the handcrafted scene-graph
rendering with end-to-end spatial perception; and (iii) distilling repetitive
intra-game decisions into lightweight RL policies via reward shaping,
shifting LLM reasoning cost from per-turn inference to a one-time training phase
and directly addressing the input token overhead identified as a current limitation.
